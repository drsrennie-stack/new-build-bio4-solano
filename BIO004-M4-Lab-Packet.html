How to use this packet

In lab

Bring Part A to every lab. Check off a structure only when you can find it on the model, the cadaver, or the slide without looking at a label. Say the name out loud to your team as you point to it.

The charts

For every muscle, the lab exam can ask where it starts (origin), where it ends (insertion), what it does (action), and which nerve supplies it. Cover the chart and quiz yourself one column at a time. Trace each flow chart with your finger on the model, then cover it and say the route from memory.

The questions

Part C shows the kinds of questions that come with a pinned or pointed structure. Practice them out loud with a partner, with the structure in front of you.

Muscles of the neck

The path of air from the nostrils to the alveoli

The conducting zone carries air and ends at the terminal bronchioles. The respiratory zone, where gases are exchanged, starts at the respiratory bronchioles.

  1. Nares (nostrils) the openings where air enters the nose
  2. Nasal vestibule just inside the nostrils; skin and coarse hairs filter large particles
  3. Nasal cavity air swirls past the superior, middle and inferior nasal conchae and is warmed, moistened and filtered
  4. Choanae the posterior openings of the nasal cavity into the pharynx
  5. Nasopharynx behind the nasal cavity, above the soft palate; carries air only
  6. Oropharynx behind the oral cavity; carries air and food
  7. Laryngopharynx from the epiglottis down to where the air and food paths divide
  8. Larynx air passes the epiglottis and goes through the glottis, the opening between the vocal folds
  9. Trachea held open by C-shaped rings of hyaline cartilage; forks at the carina
  10. Right or left main (primary) bronchus one to each lung; the right is wider and more vertical
  11. Lobar (secondary) bronchi one to each lobe: three on the right, two on the left
  12. Segmental (tertiary) bronchi one to each bronchopulmonary segment
  13. Bronchioles smooth muscle walls and no cartilage
  14. Terminal bronchioles the last airways of the conducting zone
  15. Respiratory bronchioles the first airways of the respiratory zone, with a few alveoli budding from the wall
  16. Alveolar ducts passages lined with alveoli
  17. Alveolar sacs clusters of alveoli sharing a common space
  18. Alveoli gas exchange with the pulmonary capillaries across the simple squamous wall

Lymph, from tissue fluid back to the blood

Lymph flows one way, toward the heart. Valves, skeletal muscle and breathing keep it moving.

  1. Interstitial fluid fluid that leaks out of the blood capillaries into the spaces between cells
  2. Lymphatic capillaries blind-ended tubes that take the fluid in; now it is called lymph. In the villi of the small intestine they are lacteals, which take in fat and make milky chyle
  3. Lymphatic collecting vessels carry lymph onward; many lymphatic valves stop it flowing backward
  4. Lymph nodes lymph enters through afferent lymphatic vessels, is filtered, and leaves through efferent lymphatic vessels at the hilum
  5. Lymphatic trunks collecting vessels merge into large trunks that each drain a region
  6. Right lymphatic duct or thoracic duct the right lymphatic duct drains the right arm and the right side of the head, neck and thorax. The thoracic duct begins at the cisterna chyli and drains the rest of the body
  7. Subclavian vein each duct empties where the subclavian vein meets the internal jugular vein; the right duct on the right side, the thoracic duct on the left

The path of food from the mouth to the anus

  1. Oral cavity food is chewed and mixed with saliva
  2. Oropharynx the swallowed food passes behind the mouth
  3. Laryngopharynx the epiglottis covers the larynx so food goes to the esophagus
  4. Upper esophageal sphincter a ring of skeletal muscle at the top of the esophagus
  5. Esophagus carries food down through the esophageal hiatus of the diaphragm
  6. Lower (cardiac) esophageal sphincter guards the entrance to the stomach
  7. Stomach food enters at the cardiac region, is churned in the body, and moves through the pyloric antrum, pyloric canal and pylorus
  8. Pyloric sphincter lets small amounts of food into the small intestine
  9. Duodenum the first and shortest part; bile and pancreatic juice enter at the major duodenal papilla
  10. Jejunum the middle part of the small intestine
  11. Ileum the longest part of the small intestine
  12. Ileocecal valve where the ileum joins the large intestine
  13. Cecum the pouch at the start of the large intestine; the vermiform appendix hangs from it
  14. Ascending colon up the right side
  15. Hepatic flexure (right colic flexure) the bend under the liver
  16. Transverse colon across the abdomen
  17. Splenic flexure (left colic flexure) the bend near the spleen
  18. Descending colon down the left side
  19. Sigmoid colon the S-shaped part in the pelvis
  20. Rectum the straight part in front of the sacrum
  21. Anal canal guarded by the internal and external anal sphincters
  22. Anus where feces leave the body

Blood flow through the liver

Two supplies enter the liver: the hepatic portal vein brings nutrient-rich blood from the digestive organs and the hepatic artery brings oxygen-rich blood. They mix in the sinusoids.

  1. Inferior mesenteric vein drains the descending colon, sigmoid colon and rectum; usually empties into the splenic vein
  2. Splenic vein drains the spleen and runs behind the pancreas
  3. Superior mesenteric vein drains the small intestine, cecum, ascending colon and most of the transverse colon; joins the splenic vein behind the pancreas
  4. Hepatic portal vein formed where the superior mesenteric and splenic veins join; carries nutrient-rich blood into the liver. It is a portal system because the blood passes through two capillary beds in a row: first the capillaries of the digestive organs, then the hepatic sinusoids
  5. Hepatic artery the common hepatic artery from the celiac trunk continues to the liver as the hepatic artery proper; it brings oxygen-rich blood
  6. Portal triad a branch of the hepatic portal vein and a branch of the hepatic artery enter at the corner of each lobule, beside a bile duct
  7. Hepatic sinusoids the two bloods mix and flow past the plates of hepatocytes toward the center of the lobule
  8. Central vein collects the blood in the center of each lobule
  9. Hepatic veins carry the blood out of the liver
  10. Inferior vena cava the hepatic veins empty into it, and it carries the blood to the right atrium

Bile flow from the liver to the duodenum

Bile flows toward the portal triad, the opposite way from the blood, which flows toward the central vein.

  1. Hepatocytes liver cells make bile
  2. Bile canaliculi tiny channels between the hepatocytes collect it
  3. Bile duct in the portal triad at the corner of each lobule
  4. Right and left hepatic ducts drain the right and left sides of the liver
  5. Common hepatic duct formed where the right and left hepatic ducts join
  6. Cystic duct between meals, bile backs up the cystic duct into the gallbladder
  7. Gallbladder stores and concentrates bile, then contracts after a fatty meal and sends it back out the cystic duct
  8. Common bile duct formed where the cystic duct joins the common hepatic duct
  9. Hepatopancreatic ampulla the common bile duct joins the main pancreatic duct here
  10. Sphincter of Oddi the muscle ring that opens to let bile and pancreatic juice through
  11. Major duodenal papilla the opening on the duodenal wall
  12. Duodenum bile breaks fat into small droplets

The branches of the abdominal aorta, top to bottom

The three unpaired branches (celiac trunk, superior mesenteric and inferior mesenteric arteries) supply the digestive organs. The paired branches charted here supply the kidneys and gonads.

  1. Abdominal aorta begins where the aorta passes through the aortic hiatus of the diaphragm
  2. Celiac trunk unpaired; splits into the left gastric, splenic and common hepatic arteries to supply the stomach, spleen, liver, gallbladder and parts of the pancreas and duodenum
  3. Superior mesenteric artery unpaired; supplies the small intestine, cecum, appendix, ascending colon and most of the transverse colon
  4. Renal arteries paired; arise just below the superior mesenteric artery and supply the kidneys
  5. Gonadal (testicular or ovarian) arteries paired; supply the testes or the ovaries
  6. Inferior mesenteric artery unpaired; supplies the end of the transverse colon, the descending colon, sigmoid colon and upper rectum
  7. Right and left common iliac arteries the aorta ends by splitting into these two
  8. Internal iliac artery a branch of each common iliac artery; supplies the pelvic organs, such as the bladder and the uterus or prostate
  9. External iliac artery the other branch of each common iliac artery; becomes the femoral artery as it passes under the inguinal ligament into the thigh
