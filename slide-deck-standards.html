Slide Deck Standards.

How every lecture deck in this course is built, so they all feel like the same course. The intro deck is the reference; slides-template.html is the file you copy to start a new one. Nothing here needs a paid program. A deck is one HTML file in the repo, it opens as a scrollable page on a phone and as a full-screen presentation on the projector, and every deck gets the same clock, the same click-to-zoom boxes, and the same controls.

Start here

Making a new deck, in five steps

  1. Copy slides-template.html and save it as slides-<topic>.html in the repo root, for example slides-cell-anatomy.html. Lowercase, hyphens, no spaces.
  2. Change the two LECTURE TITLE spots near the top (the browser tab title and the page header) and rewrite the title slide.
  3. Build the deck by copying whole slides from the template: title, four boxes, two boxes with a number panel, rows with dots, activity, closing. Each <section class="slide"> is one slide. Delete the ones you do not use.
  4. Add the deck to Course tools or the week page like any other page, and give it a Faculty Hub tile if you will present from it.
  5. Open it, click Present, and step through with the arrow key once before class. If anything reads stiff out loud, fix the words.
The feel

Prezi, not PowerPoint

Students do not want a wall of bullets, and they should not have to read while you talk. So every content box starts collapsed to its heading. When you click it (or press the arrow), it zooms out into a big panel with one large keyword line, the words that matter in red, and the full sentence in small type underneath. The arrow key opens the next box, and only advances the slide when every box on it has been opened, so you can run a whole lecture from a clicker. Boxes you have opened get a small "opened" mark. Press A to mark every box on a slide as seen and skip ahead.

Slides with a QR code, a grade bar, or a reference list stay static on purpose. Everything else zooms.

Anatomy of a box

Heading, keyword line, sentence

Every box has the same three parts, in this order, and the zoom depends on it:

<div class="card">
  <h3>Short heading</h3>
  <p class="kw"><b>Key words</b> in red, rest plain.</p>
  <p>The full sentence, one or two lines, in plain language.</p>
</div>

The keyword line is what the room reads. Keep it under ten words. Bold only the two or three words that carry the idea; the bold is what turns red. Write it as a real phrase, not a fragment stack: "Pull it out of memory. Don't just reread." works; "Retrieve. Don't reread." does not. The full sentence is for the student reading later on their phone.

The same three parts work in a .row (number dot on the left) and a .darkpanel (navy box for a big statistic). Rows use <div class="dot">1</div> and a wrapping <div> for the text; the template shows the exact markup.

Titles

Every title colors its key phrase

Wrap the two to four words that matter in <span class="hl">...</span>. It renders terra on white slides and gold on dark slides, automatically. One phrase per title, never the whole title. Titles are sentence case, not Title Case, and read like something you would say: "Your brain saves what it retrieves" rather than "Retrieval Practice Overview."

Above the title, the small caps kicker names the section in three or four words ("The method", "How memory actually works"). Below the boxes, the bold bigline is the one takeaway sentence, the line you would want them to remember if they remember nothing else. Use class="bigline terra" to make it terra when the slide needs a punch.

Font: Plus Jakarta Sans for everything, including small caps kickers. Weights 400, 600, 700, 800. No italics anywhere. Cards are white with a soft shadow, 8px corners for cards, no accent bars or borders on the left edge.

Words

Write it the way you would say it

The audit rule for every line: would a first-week student understand it on one read, and would you say it out loud that way? If not, rewrite. Some patterns to catch, with fixes:

"Retrieval practice strengthens encoding."
"Pulling it out of your own head is what makes it stick."
"Verified study hours, the habits that predict success."
"You earn them with verified study hours, which really do predict success in this class."
"Private writing. No talking."
"Write on your own for now, no talking yet."
"Behaviors that caused damage."
"Things people did that hurt the team."
"Meta-analysis of 225 studies."
"They combined the results of 225 studies."
"Lowest academic quartile."
"The students who came in with the weakest grades."

Full sentences, warm and direct. Say "you" and "I". No fragments stacked with periods. Spell out anything a student would have to look up (iRAT gets its full name once). Cite research on the slide in plain words ("Freeman and colleagues, 2014") and keep the exact citations for the sources slide at the end. Frame evidence around what this approach adds, never around what other formats get wrong.

Accessibility

The checks before you publish

Text on white is navy or muted, never gold. Text on navy is white or light, never muted. Every image (a QR code, a diagram) has an alt. Every box is a real button to a screen reader, and Enter or Space opens it from the keyboard. Unopened boxes dim only slightly, so they stay readable. If you add a new color pairing, check it: normal text needs 4.5:1, and every pair in the table above clears 7:1.

Do not

A short list

No paragraphs as the visible content of a box (that is what the small sentence is for). No more than four boxes per slide. No slide without a takeaway line. No paid slide tools, no exported PowerPoints for students. No student names, scores, or anything personal in a deck, since decks are public files.
