Exam season

Your Brain Needs to Practice the Test, Too

Dr. Sharilyn Rennie · Aug 24, 2026 · 4 min read

You studied. You knew this last night.

Then the exam starts, you look at a question, and suddenly your brain seems to have absolutely nothing to offer you.

That feeling is awful. And once it happens, another thought often follows: "I'm going to fail this test." Now you aren't just trying to answer a question. You are trying to answer a question while your brain is sounding an alarm.

Test anxiety is real

Anxiety can interfere with attention, working memory, and your ability to retrieve information you actually know. So when a student tells me, "I knew it before the test, but my mind went blank," I believe them.

But there is something we can do about that.

Your brain needs practice taking tests, not just practice studying for them.

If most of your studying involves notes, videos, highlighting, flashcards with hints, or looking at diagrams with labels nearby, your brain is practicing with a lot of support. Then exam day arrives. The notes disappear. The hints disappear. There is a clock. You have to commit to an answer. You don't immediately get to check whether you're right.

Of course that feels different. You haven't been practicing the same task.

Practice being tested

This is one of the reasons I give you Practice Tests and Loops. They aren't just extra assignments or something to do once before the exam.

Use them often. The more often you use them, the more opportunities your brain has to practice retrieving information under conditions that feel more like an actual exam. Don't wait until the night before the test and run through everything once. Start early. Come back to them repeatedly.

And when you use them, actually let yourself be tested. Don't immediately look something up when you aren't sure. Don't check your notes before committing to an answer. Don't restart because you don't like how you're doing.

Let yourself get stuck. Make a choice. Get some things wrong. Finish the attempt. Then go back and figure out what happened. That process matters.

The first time you encounter an unlabeled image, a difficult question, or the pressure of having to commit to an answer should not be during the exam that counts. Your Practice Tests and Loops give you a place to experience that feeling when the stakes are low. Use them to practice the material, but also use them to practice the experience of testing.

If testing makes you anxious, avoiding practice tests may feel better in the moment, but it also keeps the real exam unfamiliar. Instead, give your brain repeated experiences of: "I've been here before. I've been unsure before. I've gotten stuck before. I've recovered before. I know what to do next."

Every practice attempt gives your brain another piece of evidence that being tested is something you can handle.

What if your mind goes blank?

First, don't turn one difficult question into a prediction about the entire exam. One question you don't know means exactly that.

You don't know one question.

It does not mean you are failing. It does not mean you forgot everything. It does not mean everyone else knows it. Your brain does not need that additional story.

Reset. Read the question again. What do you know? Can you eliminate anything? Is there a word in the question that gives you information? If nothing comes, move on and come back. Sometimes another question will even trigger the information you were trying to retrieve.

Confidence comes from evidence

I can tell you, "Don't be nervous." You can tell yourself, "I'm going to do great." Those things might help a little. But there is something much more convincing.

Evidence.

"I have done this before. I have retrieved this without my notes. I have practiced with a timer. I have gotten questions wrong and figured out why. I have looked at an unlabeled structure and identified it. I have used my Practice Tests and Loops again and again. I have had my mind go blank, recovered, and kept going."

That is confidence your brain has a reason to believe.

The goal is not necessarily to walk into an exam feeling absolutely no anxiety.

The goal is to practice enough that when anxiety shows up, your brain recognizes the situation and knows what to do next.

Don't save your Practice Tests and Loops for the end of studying. Make them part of how you study from the beginning.
