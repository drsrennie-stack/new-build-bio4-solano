Why does anatomy feel so hard?

If anatomy feels hard right now, you're not alone, and it doesn't mean you're failing. Here's what the struggle means and what to do with it.

Is anatomy really this hard?

Yes. You're learning a lot of new words, and you have to find each structure in three dimensions, packed in with everything around it. It's normal for that to feel hard, especially in the first few weeks.

Does struggling mean I'm doing it wrong?

Usually, no. Learning researchers call it a desirable difficulty: when studying takes more effort in the moment, what you learn tends to stick better and last longer.

Pulling an answer out of your own memory, spreading your studying over several days, and mixing topics together all feel harder than rereading your notes. They also work better.

Why does rereading feel better if it works worse?

In one well-known study, students learned a passage of text. One group kept rereading it, and the other group practiced recalling it from memory. A week later:

61%
remembered by the students who practiced recalling
40%
remembered by the students who kept rereading

The rereading group felt more confident that they had learned it. Rereading makes material feel familiar, and familiar feels like knowing it, even when you can't pull it back out on an exam.

A large review of study methods came to the same conclusion. Practice testing and spacing your study out over time came out as the most useful methods. Rereading and highlighting came out as some of the least useful.

How do I know if it's good struggle or if I'm stuck?

Good struggle feels like reaching for an answer, getting some wrong, checking, and fixing them. It's slow, but you're making progress.

Being stuck feels like going around in circles. You've been on the same thing for a long time, you don't know where to start, or you're falling behind on the schedule.

If you're stuck, don't wait. Ask your team, post in the Virtual Office, come to office hours on Mondays and Wednesdays from 10:30 am to 12:30 pm in the conference room across from Biotech Lab 1127 or in the 30 minutes before class, or book a tutor. Getting unstuck early is much easier than catching up later.

What does this mean for how I study?

Use the tools that make you pull answers out of your memory, a little at a time over several days:

  • redraw your note-sheet drawings from memory, the way you do in a brain dump, then check them against your notes
  • practice brain dumps before you look at your notes
  • use your recall cards and Mastery OS a little every day instead of all at once
  • explain answers out loud to your team during TBLs

Each of these feels harder than rereading. That's the point, and it's why they work.

Sources: Bjork, on desirable difficulties, 1994. Roediger and Karpicke, Psychological Science, 2006. Dunlosky and colleagues, Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 2013.
