You Don't Have to Struggle Alone

Dr. Sharilyn Rennie · Aug 24, 2026 · 3 min read

Hard classes can be lonely.

You can sit in a room full of people and still feel like you are the only person who doesn't understand. Everyone else looks like they are keeping up. Someone answers a question correctly. Someone finishes the lab activity before you do. Someone seems completely confident while you are thinking, "I have absolutely no idea what is happening right now."

So you don't say anything. You go home and struggle quietly.

Sometimes we suffer in silence because we assume everyone else is doing better than we are.

Then someone says, "Wait. I don't understand this either."

And everything changes a little.

Someone else says, "I finally figured that out yesterday. Let me show you."

Suddenly, you aren't doing the hard thing alone.

That is another reason I believe so strongly in learning in teams.

It matters when someone shows up for you

Think about what it feels like when someone really shows up for you.

Someone notices you are struggling. Someone explains something without making you feel stupid for asking. Someone comes prepared when you needed them to. Someone remembers what they promised to do. Someone encourages you on a day when you were seriously questioning whether you could do this.

It feels good to know that somebody has your back.

But there is another side of that experience that I hope you discover.

It feels really good to be that person for someone else.

There is a different kind of confidence that comes from realizing, "I have something valuable to contribute here."

Maybe today you understand the brachial plexus and your teammate doesn't. Maybe someone else understands histology. Maybe you are organized. Maybe you ask really good questions. Maybe you notice the person who has stopped talking. Maybe you are the one who can make everyone laugh when the whole team is frustrated.

Being valuable to a team does not mean being the smartest person in the room. It means figuring out what you bring to the room and choosing to bring it.

And something changes when you realize other people are counting on you. You start wanting to prepare because you want to contribute. You want to know something that might help. You want to become someone your teammates are glad to see walk through the door.

Some days you will be the person who needs help. Other days you will be the person giving it. There is strength in learning how to do both.

You don't have to figure out every difficult thing by yourself. And you don't have to be the strongest person on your team to be valuable to it.

One of the things I hope you learn here has nothing to do with memorizing anatomy.

I hope you learn what it feels like when people show up for you, and how good it feels to become someone who shows up for them.
