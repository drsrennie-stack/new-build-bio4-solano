Introduction to Histology and the Microscope

Histology is where anatomy stops being something you can hold and starts being something you have to interpret. Before you can name a tissue, you have to be able to get a clean image of it. This page covers both: what histology is, and how to run the microscope well enough to trust what you are looking at.

1. What histology is

Histology is the study of tissues. The word is built from histos, meaning tissue, and logos, meaning study of. A tissue is a group of cells that share a structure and do a job together, plus whatever material those cells put around themselves.

Every organ in your body is built from four tissue types and nothing else. Once you can recognize those four, you can walk into any organ on any slide and start making sense of it.

Epithelial tissue
Covers surfaces and lines cavities. Cells packed tightly together, almost no space between them, always sitting on a basement membrane with a free surface on one side.
Connective tissue
Supports, binds, and fills. Few cells, a lot of matrix. The matrix is what you identify it by.
Muscle tissue
Contracts. Long cells, oriented in a direction, because pulling only works along a line.
Nervous tissue
Detects and signals. Cells with processes reaching out to other cells.
Why this matters

Structure predicts function at every level, and histology is the level where that link is easiest to see. A cell built to absorb looks different from a cell built to protect. Once you get used to reading structure this way, the rest of the course gets easier, because you stop memorizing and start recognizing.

2. The compound light microscope

Compound means the image gets magnified twice, once by the objective lens and again by the ocular lens. Light comes up from underneath, passes through the specimen, and carries the image to your eye. That is why your slides have to be sliced thin enough for light to get through them.

Mechanical parts

These are the parts that hold things and move things. They do not touch the image quality directly, but they are how you control what you are looking at.

Base and arm
The frame. The arm is where your hand goes when you carry it. Your other hand goes under the base.
Mechanical stage
The platform the slide sits on, with clips to hold it and two knobs that slide it left to right and front to back. Use the knobs. Do not shove the slide with your finger.
Coarse focus knob
The large knob. Moves the stage a long way fast. Scanning power only.
Fine focus knob
The small knob. Moves the stage a tiny amount. This is the only knob you touch on high power.
Revolving nosepiece
The turret the objectives are mounted in. Turn it by the ridged edge, not by grabbing an objective.

Optical parts

These are the parts that build and shape the image. If your image is bad, one of these is the reason.

Illuminator
The light source in the base, with an intensity control. Brightest is not best.
Iris diaphragm
An adjustable opening under the condenser. It controls how much light gets in, which controls contrast and depth of field. This is the most underused knob in the lab.
Condenser
A lens under the stage that gathers the light and concentrates it into a cone aimed at the specimen. Without it the light spreads out and the image goes soft.
Objective lenses
The lenses closest to the specimen. They make the first magnified image. Usually 4x scanning, 10x low power, 40x high power, and sometimes 100x oil immersion.
Ocular lenses
The eyepieces. Almost always 10x. They magnify the image the objective already made.
Two words worth knowing

Parfocal means that once you have focused on one objective, the image stays close to focus when you switch to the next one. You should only need fine focus after a switch. Parcentric means whatever was centered stays roughly centered. Both only work if you centered the specimen at low power first.

3. Magnification and resolution

These two get mixed up constantly, and they are not the same thing. Magnification is how much bigger. Resolution is how much detail.

Magnification

Total magnification is the objective power times the ocular power.

Standard lab scope, 10x oculars
ObjectiveNameTotal magnificationWhat it is for
4xScanning40xFinding the specimen and reading the overall pattern. Always start here.
10xLow power100xIdentifying tissue type and layout.
40xHigh power400xCell shape, nuclei, cell junctions, fiber detail.
100xOil immersion1000xBlood cells and bacteria. Requires immersion oil.

There is a trade you make every time you go up in power. As magnification increases, the field of view shrinks, the image gets darker, and the depth of field gets shallower. That is why a specimen you never centered at 4x disappears entirely at 40x.

Resolution

Resolution is the smallest distance two points can be apart and still be seen as two points instead of one blur. Smaller number, better resolution.

A light microscope bottoms out at roughly 0.1 to 0.2 micrometers, because visible light has a wavelength and you cannot resolve detail much finer than the wavelength you are using to look at it. Past that point, more magnification just gives you a bigger blur. That is called empty magnification, and it is why a light microscope will never show you a ribosome no matter what number you put on the box.

Say it this way

Magnification makes it bigger. Resolution makes it clearer. Resolution is the one that limits you.

4. Electron microscopes

To get past the limit of visible light, you have to stop using light. Electron microscopes fire a beam of electrons at the specimen instead. Electrons behave with a far shorter wavelength, so the resolution improves by a factor of roughly fifty to one hundred, down to about 2 nanometers, at magnifications up to about 150,000x.

The two you need to tell apart
Transmission (TEM)Scanning (SEM)
Beam does whatPasses through an ultrathin sectionSweeps across a coated surface
Image you getFlat, two dimensional, like a very detailed slideThree dimensional, like a photograph of a landscape
Shows youThe inside of a cell, organelle membranes, cristaeSurfaces and textures, microvilli, cilia, cell surfaces
PrepHeavy metal stains, plastic embedding, sections around 100 nmSpecimen coated in a thin layer of gold

An easy check on an exam image: if it looks like a black and white slice with internal detail, it is TEM. If it looks like a moon landscape with shadows and depth, it is SEM.

5. How a slide gets made

Tissue does not come off a body ready to look at. It is wet, soft, colorless, and starting to break down. Every step below exists to solve one of those four problems, and knowing which problem a step solves is the fastest way to remember the order.

  1. Fixation. Chemically stops enzymes and microbes so the tissue quits degrading and holds its structure. Solves decay.
  2. Dehydration. Water is pulled out through a series of alcohol baths of increasing strength. Water and paraffin do not mix, so the water has to go before the wax can come in.
  3. Clearing. The alcohol is replaced with xylene, which mixes with both alcohol and paraffin and makes the tissue transparent. This is the bridge step.
  4. Embedding. The tissue is infiltrated with melted paraffin, which hardens. Solves softness. You cannot cut a thin slice of something floppy.
  5. Sectioning. The block is sliced on a microtome into ribbons 5 to 10 micrometers thick, thin enough for light to pass through.
  6. Mounting. Sections are floated onto a glass slide.
  7. Staining. Dyes are applied. Solves colorless. Then a coverslip goes on.
Order mnemonic

Fixation, Dehydration, Clearing, Embedding, Sectioning, Mounting, Staining. Fix Dead Cells Every Single Monday Sharp.

Electron microscopy runs the same sequence with harsher chemistry. Fixation uses glutaraldehyde and osmium tetroxide, the block is plastic instead of wax, and the sections come out near 100 nanometers. The stains are heavy metals such as uranyl acetate and lead citrate, because electrons are stopped by dense atoms rather than absorbed by colors.

6. Stains and what they tell you

A stain is not decoration. It is chemistry. Dyes bind to structures with a particular charge, so the color on the slide is telling you something about what that structure is made of.

Almost every slide you will see this term is stained with hematoxylin and eosin, written H and E. Hematoxylin is a basic dye that binds acidic structures, above all DNA, so nuclei come out blue to purple. Eosin is an acidic dye that binds basic structures, so cytoplasm, collagen, and most extracellular material come out pink.

Vocabulary

A structure that takes up basic dye and turns blue or purple is called basophilic, meaning base loving. A structure that takes up acidic dye and turns pink is called acidophilic or eosinophilic. If you can say why a nucleus is basophilic, you understand the whole system.

7. Reading a slide well

Most people lose points on lab practicals for reasons that have nothing to do with knowing the tissue. Here is what actually goes wrong.

Read the label, then do not trust it completely
The label tells you the organ. It does not tell you which part of the field the question is about. A slide labeled trachea contains epithelium, cartilage, glands, and connective tissue. Find the part being asked about.
Look at the slide before you look through the scope
Hold it up to the light for two seconds. You will see the outline, where the tissue is, and where the good section is, and you will save a minute of hunting.
Start low, every single time
At 40x total you can see the whole section and the overall architecture. At 400x you can see a hundred cells and no context. Architecture is usually what identifies a tissue, not the cells.
Do not overilluminate
A blazing bright field washes out exactly the faint edges you need. Close the iris diaphragm until the detail sharpens. Bright is not the same as clear.
Look at several examples
One slide of a tissue teaches you that slide. Three slides teach you the tissue. Textbook images are chosen because they are perfect. Your slide is a real section from a real block and may be torn, folded, or cut at an angle.
Think in three dimensions
You are looking at a two dimensional slice through a three dimensional object. Ask how the knife went through. A tube cut across gives you a ring. The same tube cut lengthwise gives you two parallel walls. Cut at an angle it gives you an oval that can look like something else entirely. Simple columnar epithelium cut obliquely can look stratified, and that mistake costs points every term.

8. Your first five minutes at the scope

Run this sequence every time until you stop having to think about it.

  1. Carry it correctly. One hand under the base, one hand on the arm, held upright against your body. Oculars can fall out if you tip it.
  2. Set up. Plug in, turn the light on low, and clean the ocular and objective lenses with lens paper only. Paper towel scratches the coating permanently.
  3. Start on scanning. Click the 4x objective into place. You should feel and hear it seat.
  4. Lower the stage all the way with the coarse knob, then put the slide on with the coverslip facing up and secure it in the clips.
  5. Raise the stage while watching from the side, not through the oculars. Bring the objective close to the slide, then stop.
  6. Now look through the oculars and turn the coarse knob to lower the stage away until the image appears. Focusing away from the slide means you cannot drive the lens into it.
  7. Adjust the oculars to your eyes. Widen or narrow them until the two circles become one, then use the diopter ring so both eyes are sharp.
  8. Center your specimen with the mechanical stage knobs. Dead center, not near the edge.
  9. Set the light. Adjust intensity and close the iris diaphragm until edges look crispest. Do this before you go up in power, not after.
  10. Click up to 10x, then 40x, recentering each time. From 10x on, fine focus only. Never coarse focus on high power.
Shut down

Rotate back to 4x, lower the stage, remove and clean your slide, turn the light down to zero before switching off, wipe any oil off the 100x lens, wrap the cord, and put the cover on. Leaving a scope on 40x with the stage up is how the next person breaks a slide.

Troubleshooting
  • Half the field is dark. The objective is not clicked fully into place, or the stage is not centered over the condenser.
  • Specks that move when you rotate the ocular. Dirt is on the ocular lens.
  • Specks that stay put when you move the slide and the ocular. Dirt is on the objective or the condenser.
  • Image washed out and flat. Too much light. Close the iris.
  • Cannot find anything at 40x. Go back to 4x, find it, center it, and climb again.
  • Nothing focuses at all. The slide is upside down. The coverslip must face the objective.
