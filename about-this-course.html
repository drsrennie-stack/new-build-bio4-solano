About the BIO 4 Learning Environment

Course design, learning philosophy, access, and use of technology

Why this learning environment exists

The independent BIO 4 learning environment exists to improve student access, continuity, affordability, and learning support. The technology itself is not the objective. The central goal is helping students learn and retain anatomy effectively, and every part of the environment is judged against that goal.

The environment was not built in one pass. It has developed over years of teaching this material, watching how students actually learn anatomy, noticing the places where they commonly struggle, and working out which kinds of resources help them prepare, practice, retrieve information, and succeed. What exists today is the accumulation of those observations, revised each term.

Reliable access beyond Canvas

One motivation for developing an independent course environment was continuity of access. Canvas remains where announcements, assignments, and grades live, and nothing about this environment changes that. There have, however, been occasions when access to the learning management system was interrupted, including an outage close to final examinations. That experience underlined the value of students having a second, reliable way to reach essential instructional resources.

The independent environment provides that redundancy. Instructional materials are not dependent on any single system, so a service interruption in one place does not cut students off from the resources they study with.

Continued access after BIO 4

Anatomy does not end when this course does. Students commonly need this material again as they move into physiology and further health-science coursework, often within months. Because a Canvas course closes at the end of a term, one goal of the independent environment is continuity of learning: students can keep referring to the appropriate BIO 4 instructional resources after the course ends, rather than losing access on the day the section closes.

How the course content was developed

BIO 4 is not an AI-written course.

The academic content of this course is based on instructional materials personally written, developed, taught, revised, and refined by Dr. Sharilyn Rennie over approximately ten years of teaching anatomy and physiology. Those materials represent thousands of hours of her work: the course notes, the explanations, the learning activities, the laboratory materials, the practice resources, the anatomical identification practice, the review materials, the instructional sequencing, and the assessment preparation students use throughout the term.

What has changed over the years is not who writes the course. It is how the material reaches students.

How AI is used

AI is used in this course primarily as a coding, development, organization, and implementation tool. Its role is to help transform instructor-developed academic materials into an interactive, web-based learning environment: building pages, wiring navigation, generating the scaffolding of practice tools, and improving accessibility and usability.

AI-assisted tools in the environment also help students track their own learning data. The practice tools surface metrics that show each student where their gaps are, so study time can go to the specific weaknesses most likely to improve their grade. Those metrics matter because finding the gap is half of closing it; the anatomy a student is quizzed on, and the standard they are held to, remain the instructor's.

The distinction matters and is worth stating plainly. AI assists with building and improving the digital environment. It does not replace instructor authorship, subject-matter expertise, instructional judgment, or responsibility for course content. AI is not treated as an authority or a source of anatomical knowledge, and it does not determine course objectives, grading standards, or what students are expected to learn. The instructor reviews and approves the academic material presented to students and remains responsible for it.

Why build the learning resources for students?

In earlier versions of this course, students were expected to construct more of their own study infrastructure: assembling materials, building their own practice systems, and organizing the term for themselves. Over time it became clear that building all of that could consume a substantial share of a student's limited study time, and that the effort sometimes went into constructing the system rather than into practicing and retrieving anatomy.

The current environment therefore provides organized learning resources up front, so that more of a student's available time goes into using the materials: retrieving, drawing, identifying, and testing themselves. Students remain entirely free to create their own notes, flashcards, study guides, diagrams, concept maps, and practice systems. The provided environment is a resource, not a requirement that every student study identically.

Learning design

The course incorporates established learning strategies intended to support retrieval, retention, and application of anatomical knowledge, alongside the instructor's own experience of watching students succeed and struggle with this specific material.

In practice that means: retrieval practice and active recall, with tools built around getting information back out rather than rereading it; repeated and spaced practice, so material is revisited over time rather than crammed; regular practice identifying anatomical structures, which is a distinct skill from recognizing them in notes; application of knowledge through team activities and case-style questions; timely feedback, so an error is found while it can still be corrected; multiple representations of the same information, in words, images, and drawings; peer discussion through team-based learning, paired with individual accountability; and structured opportunities to revisit and correct misunderstandings after assessments.

Where the research literature and classroom experience point the same direction, the course leans in confidently. Where a choice rests mainly on the instructor's observation of past classes, it is treated as exactly that and revised when the observation changes.

Multiple ways to learn

Students do not all learn or organize information the same way, so the environment provides more than one path through the same material. The course materials library holds the notes, concept videos, workbooks, and slide decks by module. The course calendar lays the same material out day by day. Today answers the narrower question of what to do right now. Lab sprints, the Digital Atlas, image loops, and histology tools support lab identification practice, and Mastery OS provides spaced recall and self-testing. The How-To videos give short tours of each tool, and Course tools, the button in the corner of every page, reaches all of it from anywhere. Students may choose whichever of these are useful to them.

The Quick Access option

Some students prefer a plain list to an interactive environment, and the course respects that preference directly. Quick Access to Course Materials presents the required weekly materials as a straightforward list: for each week, pre-work, then notes, then videos, then lab materials, then practice, with direct links to the same files the interactive environment uses.

Students are not academically disadvantaged for preferring the simpler navigation option. The same academic expectations apply regardless of which navigation method a student uses, because both methods lead to the same materials.

Student choice

Students are welcome to use the complete interactive environment, to use Quick Access, to create their own study materials, to supplement the provided resources with a textbook, or to develop any combination that works for them. The instructor provides the learning infrastructure; students remain responsible for preparing, practicing, participating, and determining which of the available study methods serve them best.

Affordability

Affordability was a deliberate consideration in developing these resources. Commercial anatomy textbooks and interactive learning platforms can represent a significant additional expense on top of tuition. The intent here is that students have access to course-specific instructional and practice resources without being required to purchase a commercial interactive platform to succeed in this course.

The provided materials are also intentionally aligned with the content and expectations of this particular BIO 4 course, rather than being a general product adapted to it. Details of what the course does and does not require students to buy are on the required materials page.

Textbooks and academic sources

Students who prefer a traditional textbook are welcome to use one. No textbook purchase is required simply to obtain the instructional support provided in this course. For students who want a reference to look things up in, the course points to OpenStax Anatomy and Physiology 2e, a free, openly licensed textbook, and the required materials page describes its place in the course. A textbook is an available learning resource, not a gatekeeper to the course content.

Assessment philosophy

Assessment in this course is designed to balance individual preparation with team learning, and accountability with the chance to learn from mistakes. Individual readiness work and blind retrieval at the start of class reward preparation; team activities put that preparation to use through discussion and application; practice exams give low-stakes retrieval before high-stakes exams; and structured review after assessments gives students a route to find and correct what went wrong. The intent is that every assessed activity also functions as a learning activity, rather than being a measurement alone.

Continuous improvement

This environment is not static. It is revised as the instructor observes how students learn, identifies barriers, receives constructive student feedback, finds better ways to organize resources, develops new instructional approaches, and identifies opportunities to improve accessibility and usability. Changes are evaluated against one standard: whether they support learning while maintaining appropriate academic expectations. The course's accessibility practices are documented on the accessibility page.

For students

You do not need to use every feature of this system. Use the resources that help you learn. If you prefer the full interactive environment, use it. If you prefer a direct list, use Quick Access. If you prefer creating your own study materials, you may do that.

Whichever method you choose, your responsibility is the same: come prepared, practice consistently, participate, and learn the required BIO 4 material.

For faculty and course reviewers

This page documents the instructional rationale behind the independent BIO 4 learning environment. The environment is intended to support continuity of student access, long-term access to appropriate instructional resources, course-specific instructional alignment, active learning and retrieval practice, multiple pathways to the course materials, student choice, affordability, accessibility and usability, and responsible use of emerging educational technology.

Dr. Sharilyn Rennie, as the instructor of record, retains responsibility for academic content, instructional decisions, assessment, and course standards. Technology, including AI-assisted development, is used to make instructor-authored materials more accessible, useful, and supportive of student learning.
