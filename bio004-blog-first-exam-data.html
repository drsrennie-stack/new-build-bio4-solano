The First Exam Is Data, Not a Verdict

Dr. Sharilyn Rennie · Aug 24, 2026 · 3 min read

The first exam is graded, and now you have a number.

Maybe the number is what you hoped for. Maybe it isn't. If it isn't, somewhere between seeing it and closing your laptop, a sentence probably showed up in your head.

"Maybe I can't do this."

I want to catch that sentence before it settles in, because it is about to do more damage than the exam did.

One score cannot tell you whether you can do this. It can tell you what happened. Those are different things.

A verdict is final. Data is useful. A verdict says something about you. Data says something about your preparation, and preparation can be changed. You cannot change whether you are "an anatomy person." Good news: no such person exists. I have never met one. I have only met people who found a way of studying that works and people who haven't found theirs yet.

How to read your exam like data

Get your exam and go through the misses one at a time. For every question you lost, ask which kind of miss it was.

Did you never really learn it? That is a coverage problem. Something got skipped or skimmed. The fix is your study protocol, done fully, not sampled.

Did it look familiar but you couldn't produce it? That is the rereading trap. You recognized the material, but recognition is not recall. The fix is retrieval: brain dumps, recall cards, the tissue chart with the answers hidden.

Did you know it and still miss it? Slow down and look again. Misread the question, ran out of time, mixed up two structures you never practiced side by side. Each of those has its own small fix, and none of them means you don't know anatomy.

Three kinds of miss, three different fixes. A verdict gives you nothing to do except feel bad. Data gives you a list.

What the strong finishers have in common

It is not that they aced the first exam. Plenty of them didn't.

It is that they used it. They read their misses, changed one or two specific things, and walked into the next exam with a better system instead of just more worry. The students who struggle all term are usually the ones who take the number personally, study the same way but harder, and hope.

Hope is not a study strategy. Adjustment is.

And the math is on your side. This was one exam early in a long term, with lecture exams and lab practicals still ahead of you. If you want to see exactly where you stand, open the grade calculator and look at the real numbers instead of the scary imaginary ones.

Then bring your exam to office hours or to a tutor and go through the misses together. Not because you are in trouble. Because that is what the data is for.

The first exam told you where you are. It said nothing about where you are going.
