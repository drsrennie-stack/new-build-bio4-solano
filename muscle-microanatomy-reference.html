Section 1 · Laminated image 1

The sarcomere under the electron microscope

This image is a transmission electron micrograph of skeletal muscle, sliced along the length of a myofibril. The repeating light and dark bands are the striations you see on skeletal muscle under a light microscope. Each band lines up with a specific arrangement of thick filaments (myosin) and thin filaments (actin), so once you can name the bands, you can describe what the filaments are doing.

  • A

    A. M line

    What it is: the thin dark line at the dead center of the sarcomere.

    Why it matters: the M line is a protein scaffold that ties the middles of the thick filaments together. It keeps them lined up in neat rows so they all pull in the same direction instead of drifting out of position.

  • B

    B. H zone

    What it is: the paler region in the middle of the A band, on either side of the M line.

    Why it matters: the H zone contains thick filaments only, because the thin filaments do not reach this far in. During contraction the thin filaments slide toward the center, so the H zone gets narrower and can disappear entirely.

  • C

    C. A band

    What it is: the entire dark band, measured edge to edge. The H zone and M line sit inside it.

    Why it matters: the A band is exactly as long as a thick filament. Thick filaments do not shorten, so the A band keeps the same length whether the muscle is relaxed or contracted. Its outer ends look darkest because thick and thin filaments overlap there.

  • D

    D. I band

    What it is: the pale band between the A bands of neighboring sarcomeres. A Z disc runs through its middle, so half of each I band belongs to one sarcomere and half belongs to the next.

    Why it matters: the I band contains thin filaments only. As the thin filaments slide in over the thick filaments, there is less thin-only territory left, so the I band shortens during contraction.

  • E

    E. Z disc

    What it is: the dark vertical line running through the middle of the I band.

    Why it matters: thin filaments are anchored to the Z disc. Z discs mark the two ends of every sarcomere, and when the thin filaments are pulled toward the center, the Z discs are pulled closer together. That is what makes the sarcomere shorter.

  • F

    F. One sarcomere

    What it is: the segment from one Z disc to the next Z disc.

    Why it matters: the sarcomere is the functional unit of contraction, the smallest piece of a muscle fiber that can contract. A myofibril is thousands of sarcomeres linked end to end, so when each one shortens a little, the whole fiber shortens a lot.

The two pairs students mix up most

  • A band versus I band. The A band is dark and the I band is light. A quick way to keep them straight: dArk goes with the A band, and lIght goes with the I band. If you find a Z disc, you are standing in an I band.
  • M line versus H zone. The M line is the thin dark line at the exact center. The H zone is the wider pale region that surrounds it. Think of the M line as sitting in the Middle of the H zone. On the image, A points to the line itself and B points to the paler space on either side of it.

What changes when a sarcomere contracts

Compare a relaxed sarcomere with a contracted one and only some of the bands change width. Knowing which ones change, and which one never does, is the key to understanding how a muscle shortens.

The filaments slide past each other. Neither the thick nor the thin filaments get shorter, which is why the A band stays the same length while everything around it changes.
Structure During contraction Why
Sarcomere Shortens The Z discs at each end are pulled closer together.
I band Narrows Thin filaments slide in over the thick filaments, leaving less thin-only space.
H zone Narrows, can disappear Thin filaments move toward the center until they meet or overlap there.
A band No change Its length equals the length of a thick filament, and thick filaments do not shorten.

Section 2 · Laminated image 2

The skeletal muscle fiber model

The model zooms out from a single sarcomere to a whole muscle cell, also called a muscle fiber. Here you can see how the contractile parts (myofibrils and their sarcomeres) are wrapped by the membranes that deliver the signal to contract (T tubules and sarcoplasmic reticulum).

  • Myofibril

    What it is: one of the long, rod-shaped bundles packed side by side inside the fiber. Each myofibril is a chain of sarcomeres linked end to end.

    Why it matters: myofibrils are the contractile machinery of the cell. Because the sarcomeres in neighboring myofibrils line up with each other, the whole fiber looks striped.

  • Sarcoplasmic reticulum

    What it is: a network of membrane tubes that wraps around each myofibril like a lace sleeve. It is a specialized form of smooth endoplasmic reticulum.

    Why it matters: the sarcoplasmic reticulum stores calcium. Because it surrounds every myofibril, it can deliver calcium right where the filaments need it.

  • Transverse (T) tubule

    What it is: an inward fold of the sarcolemma (the muscle cell membrane) that tunnels across the fiber between the myofibrils.

    Why it matters: a muscle fiber is wide, and the signal to contract travels along its membrane. T tubules carry that signal deep into the cell so the myofibrils in the center get it at the same time as the ones near the surface.

  • Triad

    What it is: a set of three tubes: one T tubule in the middle with a terminal cistern on each side (1 T tubule + 2 terminal cisterns).

    Why it matters: the triad is the handoff point. It is where the signal carried by the T tubule reaches the calcium stores. In human skeletal muscle, triads sit where the A band meets the I band, so each sarcomere has two.

  • Sarcomere

    What it is: one repeating segment of a myofibril, from one Z disc to the next. On the model it is one unit of the light and dark banding pattern.

    Why it matters: this is the same structure you labeled F on the micrograph, now shown in place inside the cell. It is the functional unit of contraction.

  • Terminal cistern

    What it is: an enlarged sac at the end of the sarcoplasmic reticulum, pressed up against a T tubule. The plural is terminal cisterns (or cisternae).

    Why it matters: terminal cisterns hold the largest calcium store in the fiber. Sitting right beside the T tubule lets them release calcium as soon as the signal arrives.

Follow the signal through the fiber

The structures on the model work as one path. Trace it in order and the reason for each structure becomes clear.

  1. Sarcolemma. The signal to contract travels along the cell membrane.
  2. T tubule. The signal dives inward through the T tubules, deep into the fiber.
  3. Triad. The signal reaches the triads, where each T tubule sits between two terminal cisterns.
  4. Terminal cisterns release calcium. Calcium floods out around the myofibrils.
  5. Sarcomeres shorten. The filaments slide, the Z discs move closer together, and the fiber contracts.

Section 3

Check yourself

Answer each question out loud or on paper before you open it. Then open the box to compare your reasoning with the explanation.

1.Which band does not change length during contraction, and why not?Open to see the answer

The A band. Its length is set by the length of the thick filaments, and thick filaments do not shorten. During contraction the thin filaments slide in over them, so the bands around the A band (the I band and the H zone) change, but the A band itself stays the same.

If you said the I band, look again at the table: the I band narrows because thin filaments slide out of it into the overlap zone.

2.You look at a micrograph and the H zone has vanished. What does that tell you?Open to see the answer

The sarcomere is strongly contracted. The H zone is the region with thick filaments only. It disappears when the thin filaments from both sides have slid all the way to the center, so there is no thick-only space left.

A relaxed sarcomere has a wide H zone. The narrower the H zone, the further the filaments have slid.

3.Why does a skeletal muscle fiber need T tubules?Open to see the answer

To carry the signal deep into the cell. A muscle fiber is wide, and the signal to contract travels along the sarcolemma on the outside. T tubules are inward folds of that membrane, so they bring the signal to every myofibril, including the ones in the center.

Without them, the myofibrils near the surface would contract first and the deep ones would lag behind, and the fiber would not contract as a single unit.

4.Name the three parts of a triad.Open to see the answer

One T tubule in the middle and two terminal cisterns, one on each side. The T tubule brings the signal, and the terminal cisterns are the calcium stores at the ends of the sarcoplasmic reticulum.

A common mistake is listing the sarcoplasmic reticulum as a separate third part. The terminal cisterns are the part of the sarcoplasmic reticulum that belongs to the triad.

5.If you wanted to cut out exactly one sarcomere, where would you make your two cuts?Open to see the answer

Through two neighboring Z discs. A sarcomere runs from one Z disc to the next, so cutting through the middle of each one gives you exactly one.

Your piece would contain one full A band in the center, with the H zone and M line inside it, plus half of an I band at each end. That is why the I band is shared: each Z disc splits an I band between two sarcomeres.
