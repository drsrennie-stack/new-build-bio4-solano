Stop Studying Until It Looks Familiar

Dr. Sharilyn Rennie · Aug 24, 2026 · 3 min read

There is a very convincing lie your brain can tell you while you study:

"I know this."

You look at your notes and everything makes sense. You reread the chapter and recognize the terminology. You watch the lecture again and think, "Yes. I remember this." You flip over a flashcard and immediately think, "Oh yeah, I knew that."

Then I put the same concept on an exam without the clues.

And suddenly it is gone.

What happened? You may have practiced recognizing the information instead of retrieving it.

Familiar is not the same as known

Recognition is relatively easy. If I show you the answer, your brain can recognize that you have seen it before.

Retrieval is harder. Retrieval means I take the answer away and ask your brain to produce it. That is what most exams require.

And this difference can contribute to test anxiety, because you can walk into an exam genuinely believing you are prepared. You studied for hours. Everything looked familiar. Then the supports disappear and you discover that retrieving the information is much harder than recognizing it.

That is a terrible time to discover the difference.

Make your studying look more like the exam

Don't just reread the page. Close it. What did it say?

Don't stare at a labeled diagram until the structures look familiar. Cover the labels. Can you name them?

Don't immediately flip over the flashcard when you get stuck. Give your brain a few seconds to search.

Don't just watch me explain something. Pause the video. Can you explain it before I do?

And don't count something as known simply because the answer makes sense when you see it. Ask yourself: could I have produced that answer without the clue? That is a very different question.

Getting stuck is part of studying

Retrieval practice can feel worse than rereading. That's important to understand. Rereading feels productive because you recognize so much. Retrieval exposes what you don't know. You hesitate. You forget things. You get answers wrong. It can make you feel like you know less.

But you didn't suddenly lose knowledge.

You found the edge of what you actually know. That is exactly where you need to study.

I would much rather have you discover on Tuesday night that you cannot identify the branches of an artery than discover it when I point to one on Wednesday's practical. Getting something wrong while studying gives you another chance. Getting it wrong for the first time on the exam doesn't.

Don't study until you recognize everything

Study until you can retrieve it.

Close the book. Turn over the notes. Hide the labels. Put away the answer key. Give your brain the opportunity to struggle a little. Then check yourself, correct what was wrong, and do it again later.

That struggle isn't evidence that your studying isn't working. That struggle is part of what makes the learning work.

And when exam day comes, you won't be asking your brain to do something completely new. You will simply be asking it to do something it has practiced many times before.
