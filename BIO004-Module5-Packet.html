It opens with the urinary and reproductive systems, which finish the organ systems. The rest of the module is the nervous system: the brain and brainstem, the meninges and cerebrospinal fluid, the tissue and wiring logic, the spinal cord, and the nerves that run out to the body.

Kidney gross anatomy and layers

You should be able to: Describe the location and tissue layers of the kidney and identify the hilum, capsule, adipose capsule, and renal fascia.

The urinary system is made of two kidneys, two ureters, the urinary bladder, and the urethra. The kidneys filter the blood and make urine. The rest of the system carries the urine away, stores it, and releases it.

The parts of the urinary system
StructureWhat it is
KidneysThe paired organs that filter the blood and form urine
UretersThe paired tubes that carry urine from the kidneys to the bladder
Urinary bladderThe muscular sac that stores urine
UrethraThe tube that carries urine from the bladder to the outside
Roles of the kidneyFiltering wastes from the blood, balancing water, ions, and pH, and helping regulate blood pressure

Where the kidneys sit

The kidneys are bean-shaped organs on the posterior abdominal wall, one on each side of the vertebral column. They lie roughly between the T12 and L3 vertebrae. The lowest ribs partly cover their upper ends.

The kidneys are retroperitoneal. This means they sit behind the parietal peritoneum, outside the peritoneal cavity, against the muscles of the back.

The right kidney sits a little lower than the left kidney because the liver takes up the space above it. A suprarenal (adrenal) gland sits on top of each kidney like a cap. The adrenal gland is part of the endocrine system, but the same renal fascia wraps it together with the kidney.

The hilum

Each kidney has a rounded lateral border and a concave medial border. The notch in the medial border is the renal hilum. The renal artery, renal vein, nerves, lymphatic vessels, and ureter all enter or leave the kidney here. Just inside the hilum is the renal sinus, a space filled with fat that holds the renal pelvis, the calyces, and the branching vessels.

The three tissue layers

Three layers of tissue surround each kidney. From the kidney outward they are the renal capsule, the adipose capsule, and the renal fascia. Each layer has its own job.

The three layers around the kidney, from the inside out
LayerTissueJob
Renal capsule (innermost)A thin, tough layer of dense fibrous connective tissue on the kidney surfaceHolds the kidney's shape and protects its surface
Adipose capsule (middle)A layer of fat around the renal capsule, also called perirenal fatCushions the kidney and helps hold it in place
Renal fascia (outermost)A sheet of dense connective tissueAnchors the kidney and adrenal gland to the posterior abdominal wall and nearby structures

Memory tipGo from the kidney outward: capsule, fat, fascia. The capsule sits on the kidney itself, the fat cushions it, and the fascia is farthest out because it ties the whole package to the body wall.

Competency 134Lecture and lab

Internal kidney regions

You should be able to: Identify the cortex, medulla, renal columns, pyramids, papillae, parenchyma, calyces, and renal pelvis on a sectioned kidney.

When a kidney is cut in half from top to bottom (a frontal section), you see two kinds of tissue and a set of collecting spaces. The tissue that makes urine is called the parenchyma. The collecting spaces carry the finished urine to the ureter.

The renal cortex is the outer layer, just under the renal capsule. It looks grainy because it holds the renal corpuscles and the coiled tubules of the nephrons. The renal medulla lies deeper. It is made of cone-shaped renal pyramids, which look striped because tubules and collecting ducts run side by side through them.

Each pyramid has a wide base facing the cortex and a narrow tip pointing toward the renal sinus. The tip is the renal papilla. Urine drains out of tiny openings on the papilla. Between neighboring pyramids, cortex tissue reaches inward as renal columns. One pyramid together with the cortex around it makes up one lobe of the kidney.

The cortex and the medulla together form the parenchyma, the working tissue of the kidney. All of the nephrons are in the parenchyma.

Each papilla drains into a cup called a minor calyx. Several minor calyces join to form a major calyx, and the major calyces (usually two or three) join to form the renal pelvis. The renal pelvis is a funnel in the renal sinus that narrows into the ureter at the hilum.

Competency 135Lecture and lab

Nephron structure

You should be able to: Identify the renal corpuscle, glomerulus, glomerular capsule, podocytes, PCT, nephron loop, DCT, and collecting duct of a nephron.

The nephron is the microscopic unit that makes urine. Each kidney has roughly a million of them. A nephron has two main parts. The renal corpuscle filters the blood, and the renal tubule turns that filtrate into urine.

The renal corpuscle

The renal corpuscle is the filtering head of the nephron, and it always sits in the cortex. It is made of a ball of capillaries and the cup that surrounds it. The glomerulus is the ball of capillaries. Its capillary walls are fenestrated (full of small pores), which makes them very leaky.

The glomerular capsule (Bowman's capsule) is the double-walled cup around the glomerulus. Its outer parietal layer is simple squamous epithelium and forms the outside wall of the cup. Its inner visceral layer hugs the glomerular capillaries and is made of podocytes. Podocytes are cells with foot-like extensions that wrap around the capillaries. The narrow gaps between the feet are filtration slits.

The capsular space lies between the parietal and visceral layers. Filtrate collects in this space and then flows into the tubule.

The renal tubule and collecting duct

The renal tubule leaves the corpuscle as one long tube with three parts. The proximal convoluted tubule (PCT) comes first. It is coiled, stays in the cortex, and is lined with cuboidal cells that carry a brush border of microvilli.

Next is the nephron loop (loop of Henle), a U-shaped section that dips toward or into the medulla. It has a descending limb going down and an ascending limb coming back up. The loop returns to the cortex and becomes the distal convoluted tubule (DCT), a second coiled section with cuboidal cells and few microvilli.

The DCT empties into a collecting duct. Many nephrons drain into the same collecting duct, so it is shared among them. The collecting ducts run down through the pyramids and merge into larger papillary ducts, which open on the renal papilla.

  2. Capsular space The filtrate collects inside the glomerular capsule.
  3. Proximal convoluted tubule The first coiled section of the tubule.
  4. Descending limb of the nephron loop The filtrate moves down toward the medulla.
  5. Ascending limb of the nephron loop The filtrate moves back up toward the cortex.
  6. Distal convoluted tubule The second coiled section of the tubule.
  7. Collecting duct Fluid from many nephrons joins here.
  8. Papillary duct Opens on the renal papilla and drains into a minor calyx.

Memory tipThe segments are named in the order the filtrate meets them: proximal means near the corpuscle, distal means farther away. Learn them in that order and you also know the path of the filtrate.

Competency 136Lecture and lab

Cortical vs juxtamedullary nephrons

You should be able to: Compare cortical and juxtamedullary nephrons by loop depth and associated capillary beds.

Every nephron has the same parts. Nephrons differ in where the renal corpuscle sits and how deep the nephron loop reaches. The depth of the loop decides which capillary bed runs alongside the tubule.

Cortical nephrons make up about 85 percent of all nephrons. Their renal corpuscles sit in the outer part of the cortex. Their loops are short and barely dip into the outer medulla. They are wrapped by the peritubular capillaries, a mesh of capillaries that surrounds the tubules in the cortex.

Juxtamedullary nephrons make up about 15 percent. Juxta means next to, and their renal corpuscles sit in the deepest part of the cortex, right next to the medulla. Their loops are long and reach deep into the medulla, close to the renal papilla. Long, straight capillaries called the vasa recta run down and back up beside these loops. Recta means straight.

Both capillary beds are supplied by efferent arterioles leaving the glomeruli. The long loops of the juxtamedullary nephrons, together with the vasa recta, are what allow the kidney to make concentrated urine.

FeatureCortical nephronsJuxtamedullary nephrons

Nephron loopShort; barely enters the medullaLong; reaches deep into the medulla

Memory tipLong loop, long straight vessels: the juxtamedullary nephron reaches deep, so its capillaries (the vasa recta) have to reach deep with it.

Competency 137Lecture and lab

JGA and filtration membrane

You should be able to: Identify the juxtaglomerular apparatus, macula densa, and JG cells, and name the three layers of the filtration membrane and what each holds back.

The juxtaglomerular apparatus

After the nephron loop climbs back to the cortex, the tubule passes right beside its own renal corpuscle, between the afferent and efferent arterioles. The spot where the tubule and the arterioles touch is the juxtaglomerular apparatus (JGA). It checks the fluid in the tubule and the blood pressure in the arteriole, and it helps adjust filtration.

The macula densa is a patch of tall, closely packed cells in the tubule wall, where the ascending limb of the loop ends and the distal convoluted tubule begins. Macula densa means dense spot, which describes how the crowded cells look on a slide. These cells sense the amount of sodium chloride in the fluid passing by.

The juxtaglomerular cells (JG cells, also called granular cells) are modified smooth muscle cells, mostly in the wall of the afferent arteriole. Their granules hold the enzyme renin, which they release when blood pressure drops.

The juxtaglomerular apparatus
StructureWhere it isWhat it does
Juxtaglomerular apparatusWhere the tubule touches the afferent and efferent arterioles of its own corpuscleMonitors the tubule fluid and the arteriole and helps adjust filtration
Macula densaTubule wall, where the ascending limb meets the distal convoluted tubuleSenses the sodium chloride level of the fluid
Juxtaglomerular (granular) cellsWall of the afferent arterioleRelease renin when blood pressure is low

The filtration membrane

Blood in the glomerulus is filtered into the capsular space across the filtration membrane. Fluid crosses three layers in a set order, and each layer holds back a different size of particle.

The three layers of the filtration membrane, from the blood side to the capsular space
LayerWhat it isWhat it holds back
Fenestrated endotheliumThe pored wall of the glomerular capillaryBlood cells and platelets
Basement membraneThe layer between the capillary wall and the podocytesLarge plasma proteins
Filtration slits of the podocytesThe gaps between podocyte feet, bridged by thin slit membranesMost of the smaller proteins that get past the basement membrane

What passes all three layers is the filtrate: water and small dissolved substances such as ions, glucose, and wastes like urea. Healthy filtrate has almost no protein and no blood cells. Protein or blood in the urine is a sign that the filtration membrane is damaged.

Memory tipEach layer catches something smaller than the one before it: cells first, then big proteins, then the smaller proteins.

Competency 138Lecture and lab

Kidney blood supply pathway

You should be able to: Trace blood through the kidney from the renal artery to the renal vein, naming each vessel in order.

The kidneys receive about a quarter of the blood the heart pumps at rest. Blood follows a fixed chain of arteries inward, passes through two capillary beds, and leaves through a chain of veins that runs back along the same route.

The names of the vessels tell you where they run. A pyramid with its surrounding cortex is a lobe, so interlobar vessels run between lobes, up through the renal columns. Arcuate means arched, and these vessels arch over the bases of the pyramids at the border between the cortex and medulla. Cortical radiate vessels radiate outward into the cortex. Some books call them interlobular vessels.

  1. Renal artery Branches off the abdominal aorta and enters the kidney at the hilum.
  2. Segmental arteries Branches of the renal artery inside the renal sinus.
  3. Interlobar arteries Run up through the renal columns, between the pyramids.
  4. Arcuate arteries Arch over the bases of the pyramids, along the border between cortex and medulla.
  5. Cortical radiate arteries Also called interlobular arteries; radiate outward into the cortex.
  6. Afferent arteriole Carries blood into one glomerulus.
  7. Glomerulus The first capillary bed, where blood is filtered.
  8. Efferent arteriole Carries blood out of the glomerulus.
  9. Peritubular capillaries and vasa recta The second capillary bed. Peritubular capillaries wrap the tubules in the cortex, and the vasa recta run beside the long loops in the medulla.
  10. Cortical radiate veins Drain the cortex and run back toward the medulla.
  11. Arcuate veins Arch over the bases of the pyramids.
  12. Interlobar veins Run down through the renal columns toward the renal sinus.
  13. Renal vein Leaves the kidney at the hilum and empties into the inferior vena cava.

This path is unusual in one way. In most of the body, a capillary bed drains into a venule. The glomerulus has an arteriole on both sides, the afferent arteriole going in and the efferent arteriole coming out. Blood then passes through a second capillary bed before it reaches any vein.

Memory tipBlood meets two capillary beds in the kidney, one after the other: the glomerulus, where filtration happens, and then the peritubular capillaries and vasa recta around the tubules. The efferent arteriole is the vessel between them. On the way out, the veins repeat the artery names in reverse: cortical radiate, arcuate, interlobar, renal.

Competency 139Lecture and lab

Ureters, bladder, urethra and urine path

You should be able to: Trace urine from the collecting duct to the outside and identify the ureter, bladder wall layers, trigone, detrusor, sphincters, and male and female urethra.

Once fluid leaves the collecting duct it is urine, and nothing along the rest of the path changes it much. The rest of the system collects the urine, carries it, stores it, and releases it.

The path of urine

  1. Collecting duct Gathers fluid from many nephrons and carries it through the pyramid.
  2. Papillary duct Opens on the renal papilla.
  3. Minor calyx Cup that catches urine from one papilla.
  4. Major calyx Forms where several minor calyces join.
  5. Renal pelvis Funnel that collects urine from the major calyces.
  6. Ureter Carries urine down to the bladder.
  7. Urinary bladder Stores the urine.
  8. Urethra Carries urine out of the body.
  9. External urethral orifice The opening to the outside.

The ureters

Each ureter is a muscular tube about 30 cm long. It leaves the renal pelvis at the hilum, runs down the posterior abdominal wall behind the peritoneum, and enters the posterior wall of the bladder. The ureter passes through the bladder wall at a slant. When the bladder fills, pressure squeezes this slanted part closed, which keeps urine from flowing back up toward the kidney.

The ureter wall has three layers. The inner mucosa is lined with transitional epithelium (urothelium), which can stretch. The middle layer is smooth muscle, which pushes urine along by peristalsis. The outer layer is a fibrous adventitia.

The urinary bladder

The urinary bladder is a hollow, muscular sac in the pelvic cavity, just behind the pubic symphysis. In males it sits in front of the rectum and above the prostate. In females it sits in front of the vagina and below the uterus. Only its upper surface is covered by peritoneum.

The layers of the bladder wall, from the inside out
LayerWhat it isKey feature
MucosaTransitional epithelium (urothelium) on a layer of connective tissue (lamina propria)Stretches as the bladder fills; folds into rugae when the bladder is empty
Detrusor muscleThe muscular layer, made of layers of smooth muscleContracts to squeeze urine out
AdventitiaOuter fibrous connective tissueOver the upper surface, the peritoneum (a serosa) covers the bladder

The trigone is a smooth triangle on the floor of the bladder. Its three corners are the two ureteral openings at the back and the internal urethral orifice at the front, where the urethra begins. The trigone has no rugae, so it stays smooth even when the bladder is empty.

Two sphincters control the outflow. The internal urethral sphincter is a thickening of smooth muscle at the neck of the bladder, where the urethra begins. It is involuntary. The external urethral sphincter is skeletal muscle that surrounds the urethra as it passes through the pelvic floor. It is voluntary and gives conscious control over urination.

The urethra

The urethra carries urine from the bladder to the outside. It is very different in females and males.

Competency 140Lecture

Clinical anatomy of urinary disorders

You should be able to: Relate urinary disorders such as nephroptosis, kidney stones, and UTI to the anatomical structure or pathway each one affects.

Urinary disorders make the most sense when you place each one on the structures you already know. Some damage the filtering parts of the nephron. Others block, infect, or weaken the tubes that carry and hold urine.

Any blockage along the path makes urine back up into the parts above it. When urine backs up into the renal pelvis and calyces, they swell. This swelling is called hydronephrosis.

Urinary disorders and the structures they affect
DisorderStructure or pathway affectedWhat changes
NephroptosisAdipose capsule and renal fascia; the ureter belowThe kidney drops out of its normal position, often when its cushion of fat is thin or lost. The ureter can kink and block urine flow.
Kidney stones (renal calculi)Form in the calyces and renal pelvis; lodge in the ureterMinerals harden into stones. A stone tends to stick at the narrow points of the ureter: where it leaves the renal pelvis, where it crosses the iliac vessels at the pelvic brim, and where it enters the bladder.
Urinary tract infectionUrethra, bladder, and, if it climbs, the ureter and kidneyBacteria, most often from the intestine (such as E. coli), climb up the urine path and inflame its lining. Infection of the bladder is cystitis. Infection that reaches the renal pelvis and kidney tissue is pyelonephritis.
GlomerulonephritisGlomeruliThe glomeruli become inflamed, which damages the filtration membrane. Protein and blood can leak into the urine.
Renal failureNephrons of both kidneysThe kidneys lose their ability to filter the blood, either suddenly (acute) or slowly over time (chronic).
Polycystic kidney diseaseRenal parenchyma (the tubules)An inherited disorder in which many fluid-filled cysts form and enlarge the kidneys, crowding out working tissue.
Urinary incontinenceDetrusor muscle, urethral sphincters, and pelvic floorLoss of voluntary control over urination. In stress incontinence a weak pelvic floor and external sphincter leak with coughing or lifting. In urge incontinence the detrusor contracts too readily. In overflow incontinence the bladder cannot empty fully, so it overfills and leaks. In functional incontinence the urinary organs work, but the person cannot reach a toilet in time.

Position and pathway predict the problem

The kidney depends on its adipose capsule to stay in place. When that fat is lost, the kidney can drop, and the ureter can kink and block the flow of urine.

Urine travels one narrow path. A kidney stone often causes little pain while it sits in the renal pelvis. Pain usually starts when the stone moves into the ureter, where it scrapes and blocks a tube only a few millimeters wide. Where the stone sits along that path shapes where the pain is felt.

The two urethras differ, and so does the risk. The female urethra is short and opens close to the anus, which gives bacteria a shorter climb to the bladder. This is one reason urinary tract infections are more common in women.

Scrotum and testis structure

You should be able to: Identify the scrotum, dartos and cremaster muscles, tunica layers, seminiferous tubules, sustentacular and interstitial cells, rete testis, and efferent ductules.

The male reproductive system makes sperm and delivers them. Its parts sort into four groups: the gonads, a duct system, the accessory glands, and the supporting structures. This section covers the gonads, the testes, and the pouch that holds them.

The four groups of parts
GroupWhat it includes
TestesThe male gonads; they produce sperm and secrete hormones
Duct systemThe epididymis, ductus deferens, ejaculatory ducts, and urethra, which store, mature, and carry sperm
Accessory glandsThe seminal vesicles, prostate, and bulbourethral glands, which add fluid to form semen
Supporting structuresThe scrotum and the penis

The scrotum and its two muscles

The scrotum is a pouch of skin and subcutaneous tissue that hangs behind the penis, outside the pelvic cavity. A ridge down its middle, the raphe, marks where the scrotal septum divides the inside into two compartments, one for each testis. The testes sit outside the body because sperm production needs a temperature a few degrees below the body core.

Two muscles adjust that temperature, and they are different tissues. The dartos muscle is a thin layer of smooth muscle in the subcutaneous tissue of the scrotum. When it contracts, the scrotal skin wrinkles and tightens, which cuts heat loss. The cremaster muscle is skeletal muscle. It comes off the internal oblique muscle of the abdominal wall, travels down in the spermatic cord, and loops around each testis. When it contracts, it pulls the testis up toward the warmer body wall. In warm conditions both muscles relax and the testes hang lower.

The coverings of the testis

Each testis is an oval organ wrapped in two layers called tunics. The outer one, the tunica vaginalis, is a serous membrane with a parietal layer and a visceral layer. It comes from a pouch of peritoneum that the testis brought with it when it moved down into the scrotum before birth, and it covers the front and sides of the testis. Under it is the tunica albuginea, a tough white capsule of dense connective tissue. The tunica albuginea sends thin walls (septa) inward that divide the testis into a few hundred wedge-shaped lobules.

Inside the testis

Each lobule holds tightly coiled seminiferous tubules. These tubules are the only place sperm are made. On a slide, a seminiferous tubule is a round or oval profile with a thick wall of cells around a small central lumen. The developing sperm cells are stacked in that wall, the least mature at the outer edge and the most mature next to the lumen.

Sustentacular cells (Sertoli cells) are tall cells that reach from the outer edge of the tubule to its lumen. They surround, support, and nourish the developing sperm cells. Tight junctions between neighboring sustentacular cells form the blood-testis barrier, which keeps substances in the blood away from the developing sperm. Interstitial cells (Leydig cells) sit in the connective tissue between the tubules, outside the tubule walls. They produce testosterone.

From the rete testis, a set of tiny efferent ductules leave the testis and carry the sperm into the head of the epididymis.

Sperm duct system and pathway

You should be able to: Trace the path of sperm from the seminiferous tubules to the outside, naming the epididymis, ductus deferens, ejaculatory duct, urethra, and spermatic cord contents.

Only the seminiferous tubules make sperm. Every duct after them stores, matures, or carries the sperm. The route runs in one direction: up out of the scrotum, into the pelvis behind the bladder, and then down through the prostate and the penis to the outside.

Epididymis

The epididymis is a comma-shaped structure on the posterior side of each testis. Its head sits at the top, where the efferent ductules enter. Its body runs down the back of the testis, and its tail sits at the bottom. Inside is a single duct about 6 meters long, coiled tightly enough to fit in a few centimeters. Sperm are stored here, and they gain the ability to swim as they move through it.

Ductus deferens

The ductus deferens (vas deferens) begins at the tail of the epididymis. It is a long duct with a thick wall of smooth muscle, so it feels like a firm cord through the scrotal skin. It climbs in the spermatic cord, passes through the inguinal canal into the pelvic cavity, crosses over the ureter, and runs down the posterior surface of the bladder. Its last part widens into the ampulla of the ductus deferens.

Ejaculatory duct and urethra

Behind the bladder, each ampulla joins the duct of a seminal vesicle. The two together form the short ejaculatory duct. There is one on each side. Each ejaculatory duct passes through the prostate and empties into the urethra.

The male urethra carries both urine and semen. It has three parts. The prostatic urethra runs through the prostate, just below the bladder. The membranous urethra is the short part that passes through the muscles of the pelvic floor (the urogenital diaphragm). The spongy urethra (penile urethra) is the longest part. It runs through the corpus spongiosum of the penis and opens at the external urethral orifice on the glans.

Spermatic cord

The spermatic cord is the bundle that suspends each testis and carries its duct, vessels, and nerves between the scrotum and the abdomen. It passes through the inguinal canal, a slanted passage through the lower anterior abdominal wall. The cord holds these structures:

  • The ductus deferens
  • The testicular artery, a branch of the abdominal aorta
  • The pampiniform plexus, a network of veins wrapped around the testicular artery; it cools the blood flowing in to the testis, and its veins join to form the testicular vein
  • Lymphatic vessels and nerves
  • The cremaster muscle and layers of fascia that wrap the whole cord

Sperm cell structure

You should be able to: Identify the head, acrosome, neck, middle piece, principal piece, and end piece of a sperm cell.

A mature sperm cell (spermatozoon) is built for one job: reaching an oocyte and delivering its chromosomes. It has two main parts, a head and a tail. The tail is a long flagellum. From front to back, the tail has four parts: the neck, the middle piece, the principal piece, and the end piece.

The head is a flattened oval. Most of it is the nucleus, which holds 23 chromosomes, half the number in a body cell. There is almost no cytoplasm. The acrosome is a cap that covers the front of the nucleus. It is a sac of enzymes that lets the sperm digest a path through the layers around the oocyte.

The neck is the short, narrow part just behind the head. It holds the centrioles, and the core of microtubules that runs the length of the tail starts here.

The middle piece is packed with mitochondria wound in a spiral around the core of the tail. They make the ATP that powers the tail. The principal piece is the longest part of the tail and does most of the whipping that moves the cell forward. The end piece is the short, thin tip at the very end of the tail.

On a slide of the testis, you see sperm heads and tails crowded in the lumen of the seminiferous tubules. On a sperm smear, the head is the dark, densely stained oval, and the tail is the long thin line behind it.

Accessory glands and penis

You should be able to: Name the seminal vesicles, prostate, and bulbourethral glands and identify the root, body, glans, prepuce, and three erectile columns of the penis.

The accessory glands

Sperm are only a small part of semen. Three sets of accessory glands add most of the fluid. The mix of sperm and these secretions is semen. Where each gland sits tells you where its fluid joins the duct system.

The seminal vesicles are a pair of glands on the posterior surface of the bladder, beside the ampullae of the ductus deferens. The duct of each seminal vesicle joins the ductus deferens to form the ejaculatory duct. Their fluid is alkaline and rich in fructose, a sugar the sperm use for energy. It makes up about 60 percent of the volume of semen.

The prostate is a single gland about the size of a walnut. It sits just below the bladder and wraps around the prostatic urethra. Its back surface lies against the rectum. Many small ducts carry its milky fluid into the prostatic urethra, where it helps the sperm stay active.

The bulbourethral glands (Cowper glands) are a pair of pea-sized glands just below the prostate, beside the membranous urethra in the pelvic floor. Their ducts open into the spongy urethra. They release a clear mucus before ejaculation that neutralizes traces of acidic urine in the urethra and lubricates the tip of the penis.

Clinical anatomy of male disorders

You should be able to: Relate male reproductive disorders such as cryptorchidism and benign prostatic hyperplasia to the affected structure and its position.

Most male reproductive disorders make sense once you know where the structure sits. The testes form inside the abdomen and move down into the scrotum. The prostate wraps around the urethra. The veins of the testis run up a long cord. Each of these positions explains a disorder.

Before birth, the testes develop high on the posterior abdominal wall, near the kidneys. That is why each testicular artery branches from the abdominal aorta. Late in fetal development, each testis moves down and passes through the inguinal canal into the scrotum, pulling its duct and vessels behind it. In cryptorchidism, one or both testes stop along this route, in the abdomen or in the inguinal canal. An undescended testis stays at core body temperature, which impairs sperm production and raises the risk of testicular cancer.

The prostate sits just below the bladder and surrounds the prostatic urethra. In benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH), the prostate enlarges without cancer, most often in the tissue right around the urethra. The enlarged gland squeezes the prostatic urethra, so the urine stream weakens and the bladder may not empty fully. BPH is common in older men. Prostate cancer most often starts in the outer, posterior part of the gland. Because the back of the prostate lies against the rectum, a clinician can feel it through the rectal wall.

The inguinal canal is a weak spot in the abdominal wall, left by the descent of the testis. In an inguinal hernia, a loop of intestine pushes into the canal and can follow the spermatic cord down into the scrotum. In a hydrocele, serous fluid collects between the two layers of the tunica vaginalis. In a varicocele, the veins of the pampiniform plexus swell and twist. Varicoceles are more common on the left, where the testicular vein drains into the left renal vein.

Why the testes hang outside

Sperm production needs a temperature a few degrees below the body core. That is why the testes sit in the scrotum and why the cremaster and dartos muscles adjust their position. In cryptorchidism, an undescended testis stays at body temperature, and if it is not corrected the warmth can damage the sperm-forming cells.

The duct system shares its final stretch with the urinary tract. Because the urethra passes straight through the prostate, an enlarged prostate squeezes the urethra and makes urination difficult. This is how a reproductive organ produces a urinary symptom.

Where the ovaries sit and what holds them

The ovaries are the paired female gonads. Each is an almond-shaped organ against the lateral wall of the pelvic cavity, one on each side of the uterus, just below the open end of a uterine tube. Three ligaments hold each ovary in place.

The broad ligament is a large fold of peritoneum that drapes over the uterus, the uterine tubes, and the ovaries. The ovarian ligament is a cord of fibrous tissue that ties the ovary to the side of the uterus, just below where the uterine tube joins it. The layers of the ovary

Read the ovary from the outside in. The germinal epithelium (ovarian surface epithelium) is a single layer of cuboidal cells covering the ovary. Despite its name, it does not make oocytes. Under it is the tunica albuginea, a capsule of dense connective tissue, with the same name as the capsule of the testis. Next is the ovarian cortex, which holds the follicles. The center is the ovarian medulla, loose connective tissue with the blood vessels, lymphatic vessels, and nerves.

The follicle stages

An ovarian follicle is an oocyte plus the cells around it. The cells next to the oocyte are follicle cells, called granulosa cells once they become cuboidal. In later stages, connective tissue cells outside them form a layer called the theca. Follicles grow through a set series of stages, so on a slide you name each one by its size, its layers of cells, and whether it has a fluid space.

A primordial follicle is a primary oocyte with one layer of flat follicle cells. These are the smallest and most numerous follicles, in the outer cortex. In a primary follicle, the follicle cells become cuboidal granulosa cells and a clear layer, the zona pellucida, forms around the oocyte. A secondary follicle has several layers of granulosa cells, a theca around the outside, and small pockets of fluid between the granulosa cells. A mature follicle (vesicular or Graafian follicle) has one large fluid-filled cavity, the antrum. Its oocyte sits on a mound of granulosa cells to one side, ringed by the corona radiata. It is big enough to bulge from the surface of the ovary. Just before ovulation, its oocyte becomes a secondary oocyte.

At ovulation, the mature follicle ruptures and releases the secondary oocyte with its corona radiata. The granulosa and theca cells left behind become the corpus luteum (yellow body), a large glandular body that secretes hormones. If no pregnancy occurs, the corpus luteum breaks down into the corpus albicans (white body), a pale scar of fibrous tissue. Fluid-filled ovarian cysts often form from a follicle or a corpus luteum that does not go away.

Uterine tubes and ovum pathway

You should be able to: Trace the oocyte from the ovary to the uterus, naming the fimbriae, infundibulum, ampulla, and isthmus.

The uterine tubes (fallopian tubes) are a pair of tubes about 10 cm long. Each runs from an upper corner of the uterus out toward an ovary, inside the upper edge of the broad ligament. This part of the broad ligament is the mesosalpinx. The tube carries the oocyte toward the uterus, and it is where fertilization usually happens.

The outer end of the tube is not attached to the ovary. At ovulation the oocyte is released into the pelvic cavity, and the tube has to catch it. The tube has four named regions, from the ovary toward the uterus.

The infundibulum is the funnel-shaped outer end, open to the pelvic cavity near the ovary. Its edge is fringed with fimbriae, finger-like projections that drape over the ovary. Around ovulation the fimbriae sweep across the surface of the ovary and draw the oocyte into the funnel. The ampulla is the widest and longest part of the tube. Fertilization usually happens here. The isthmus is the narrow, thick-walled part that joins the uterus. A short intramural part then passes through the uterine wall and opens into the uterine cavity.

The lining of the tube has ciliated cells and secretory cells. The cilia beat toward the uterus, and a layer of smooth muscle in the wall squeezes in waves. Together they move the oocyte, or the early embryo, toward the uterus over a few days.

An open pathway to the pelvic cavity

The female tract is open to the pelvic cavity. The infundibulum opens near the ovary and does not seal onto it. That opening is how the fimbriae catch an oocyte. In an ectopic pregnancy, an embryo implants outside the uterine cavity. Most implant in the uterine tube, usually in the ampulla. This happens when the embryo is held up inside the tube, for example where scarring from earlier PID narrows it. The thin wall of the tube cannot stretch to hold a growing embryo. In rare cases an embryo implants in the pelvic or abdominal cavity, which is possible because the infundibulum is open.

Vagina and external genitalia

You should be able to: Identify the vagina, fornix, hymen, and the vulvar structures including the clitoris, vestibule, labia, and greater vestibular glands.

The vagina

The vagina is a thin-walled fibromuscular tube about 10 cm long. It runs from the cervix down and forward to the outside. It lies behind the bladder and urethra and in front of the rectum. It receives the penis, carries menstrual flow out, and serves as the birth canal. Its lining is stratified squamous epithelium in folds called rugae, with a layer of smooth muscle around it. It opens to the outside at the vaginal orifice.

The cervix pokes into the top of the vagina. The recess that rings the cervix is the fornix. Its posterior part, the posterior fornix, is the deepest. The hymen is a thin fold of mucous membrane at the vaginal orifice that partly covers it. Its shape and size vary a great deal from person to person.

The vulva

The external genitalia together are the vulva. The mons pubis is a rounded pad of fat over the pubic symphysis. The labia majora are two folds of skin and fat that run back from the mons pubis. They are covered with hair after puberty. The labia minora are two thin, hairless folds inside the labia majora. At the front, they meet over the clitoris and form its prepuce.

The clitoris is a small erectile organ at the front of the vulva, where the labia minora meet. Like the penis, it is built of erectile tissue, and only its tip, the glans of the clitoris, shows on the surface. The vestibule is the space between the labia minora. It holds the two main openings: the external urethral orifice toward the front and the vaginal orifice behind it.

The greater vestibular glands (Bartholin glands) are a pair of pea-sized glands, one on each side of the vaginal orifice, slightly toward the back. Their ducts open into the vestibule, and their mucus lubricates the vestibule. The perineum is the diamond-shaped region between the pubic symphysis and the coccyx. Its front half, the urogenital triangle, holds the external genitalia. Its back half, the anal triangle, holds the anus.

Mammary gland structure

You should be able to: Identify the lobes, lobules, alveoli, lactiferous ducts, nipple, areola, and suspensory ligaments of the mammary gland.

The mammary glands are modified sweat glands in the breasts. Each breast lies in the subcutaneous tissue in front of the pectoralis major muscle. Much of the breast is adipose tissue, and the amount of fat mostly sets the size of the breast.

The gland is built in nested units. It has 15 to 20 lobes arranged in a ring around the nipple, separated by fat. Each lobe divides into smaller lobules. Each lobule holds clusters of alveoli, small sacs lined with cells that secrete milk. Myoepithelial cells around the alveoli contract to push the milk into the ducts.

Small ducts from the alveoli join into one lactiferous duct for each lobe. Just before the nipple, each lactiferous duct widens into a lactiferous sinus, where milk collects. The ducts open on the nipple, the raised projection near the center of the breast. The areola is the ring of pigmented skin around the nipple. Its small bumps are areolar glands, a type of sebaceous gland.

The suspensory ligaments (Cooper ligaments) are bands of connective tissue that run from the skin through the breast, between the lobes, to the fascia over the pectoralis major. They support the breast. Most breast cancer starts in the ducts. A tumor that pulls on a suspensory ligament can dimple the skin over it.

Brain gross organization

You should be able to: Identify the four major brain divisions cerebrum, diencephalon, brainstem, and cerebellum, and distinguish the cortical gray matter from the deep white matter on a specimen.

The brain sits in the cranial cavity. At the foramen magnum it continues downward as the spinal cord. Anatomists divide the brain into four major divisions, and you can find all four on a midsagittal section of the brain model.

The cerebrum is the largest division. It forms the two cerebral hemispheres that fill the upper cranial cavity. The diencephalon sits at the core of the brain, between the hemispheres and above the brainstem. The hemispheres cover it, so you see it best on a midsagittal cut. The brainstem is the stalk below the diencephalon. It is made of the midbrain, pons, and medulla oblongata, and it continues as the spinal cord. The cerebellum sits behind the brainstem and below the occipital lobes, in the posterior cranial fossa.

The brain is built from two kinds of nervous tissue. Gray matter holds neuron cell bodies, dendrites, unmyelinated axons, and synapses. It looks gray to tan. White matter is made of myelinated axons running in bundles called tracts. Myelin is fatty, so white matter looks pale.

In the cerebrum, gray matter forms a thin outer layer called the cerebral cortex. It is only about 2 to 4 mm thick, and it follows every gyrus and sulcus. Beneath it lies the cerebral white matter, the deep core of tracts. Islands of gray matter also sit deep inside the white matter. These are the basal nuclei and the thalamus. The cerebellum uses the same plan: gray cortex on the outside and white matter inside. The brainstem and spinal cord are arranged the other way around, with most of their white matter on the outside.

On a specimen, look at a coronal or horizontal slice. The cortex is the thin, darker rim that traces the outline of each fold. The pale tissue deep to it is white matter. Darker patches buried inside the white matter are the deep nuclei.

Memory tipCortex is Latin for bark. Like bark on a tree, the cortex is a thin outer layer, and the white matter is the wood inside.

Neural tube brain development

You should be able to: Trace brain development from the neural tube through the primary and secondary vesicles and match each secondary vesicle to its adult structures and ventricle.

The whole central nervous system develops from the neural tube, which forms from ectoderm. In the early embryo a strip of ectoderm called the neural plate folds into a groove, and the edges of the groove fuse into a tube. The front (cranial) end of the tube becomes the brain. The rest of the tube becomes the spinal cord.

The tube stays hollow. Its central cavity becomes the ventricles of the brain and the central canal of the spinal cord. So as you follow each part of the tube into the adult brain, you also follow its cavity into a ventricle.

By about the fourth week, the cranial end of the tube swells into three primary brain vesicles: the prosencephalon (forebrain), the mesencephalon (midbrain), and the rhombencephalon (hindbrain).

By about the fifth week, two of these split, giving five secondary brain vesicles. The prosencephalon divides into the telencephalon and the diencephalon. The mesencephalon does not divide. The rhombencephalon divides into the metencephalon and the myelencephalon.

Cerebral surface and lobes

You should be able to: Identify the cerebral hemispheres, five lobes, gyri, sulci, and the named central, lateral, and parieto-occipital sulci along with the longitudinal and transverse fissures on a specimen.

The cerebrum is made of two cerebral hemispheres, right and left. A deep midline cleft called the longitudinal fissure separates them. At the bottom of that fissure, the two hemispheres are joined by the corpus callosum. A second deep cleft, the transverse fissure, separates the back of the cerebrum from the cerebellum below it.

The surface of each hemisphere is folded. The raised ridges are gyri (singular, gyrus). The grooves between them are sulci (singular, sulcus). The deepest grooves are called fissures. Folding packs much more cortex into the skull than a smooth surface could hold.

A few sulci are named because they mark the lobe boundaries. The central sulcus runs from the top of the hemisphere down the side toward the lateral sulcus. The precentral gyrus lies just in front of it, bounded in front by the precentral sulcus. The postcentral gyrus lies just behind it, bounded behind by the postcentral sulcus. The lateral sulcus (Sylvian fissure) is the deep groove on the side of each hemisphere. The parieto-occipital sulcus is easiest to see on the medial surface, near the back of the hemisphere.

Functional cortical areas

You should be able to: Map the motor, sensory, and association areas of the cortex to their gyri and lobes, including primary motor, primary somatosensory, primary visual, auditory, gustatory, and olfactory cortices, Broca and Wernicke areas, and the prefrontal cortex.

The cortex is mapped into areas that each have a fixed location. There are three kinds. Motor areas control voluntary movement and sit in the frontal lobe. Sensory areas are the first cortex to receive a given kind of sensation. A primary sensory area receives that input, and an association area beside it interprets it. Association areas make up the rest of the cortex. They interpret input, connect it with memory, and plan responses.

Motor areas, frontal lobe

The primary motor cortex is the precentral gyrus. Its neurons send commands for voluntary movement to skeletal muscles on the opposite side of the body. The body is mapped along the gyrus upside down. The leg and foot are on the medial surface, inside the longitudinal fissure. The hand is higher on the lateral surface, and the face is lowest, near the lateral sulcus. Body parts with fine control, such as the hand, lips, and tongue, get the largest share of cortex. The premotor cortex lies just in front of the precentral gyrus and plans learned, patterned movements.

Broca's area sits in the inferior frontal gyrus, usually in the left hemisphere. It plans the movements of speech.

Sensory areas

The primary somatosensory cortex (primary sensory cortex) is the postcentral gyrus of the parietal lobe. It receives touch, pressure, pain, temperature, and body position from the opposite side of the body. Its body map is laid out in the same order as the motor map next to it. The somatosensory association area lies just behind it.

The special senses each have their own primary area. The primary visual cortex is in the occipital lobe, along the calcarine sulcus on the medial surface. The primary auditory cortex is on the superior temporal gyrus, along the lower edge of the lateral sulcus. The primary gustatory cortex (taste) is in the insula and the lower end of the postcentral gyrus that covers it. The primary olfactory cortex (smell) is on the medial surface of the temporal lobe, near the uncus. Smell reaches it from the olfactory bulb and olfactory tract, which lie on the inferior surface of the frontal lobe.

Association areas

Wernicke's area sits in the posterior part of the superior temporal gyrus, where it meets the parietal lobe, usually in the left hemisphere. It is needed to understand spoken and written language. A tract called the arcuate fasciculus connects it to Broca's area. The prefrontal cortex covers the front of the frontal lobe. It is a large association area that handles planning, judgment, and personality.

Cerebral white matter tracts

You should be able to: Compare association, commissural, and projection fibers by what each connects and identify the corpus callosum and internal capsule on a specimen.

The cerebral white matter is made of myelinated axons grouped into tracts. The tracts fall into three classes, sorted by what they connect.

Association fibers stay within one hemisphere. Short ones arc from one gyrus to the next. Long ones connect one lobe to another. The arcuate fasciculus, which links Broca's area and Wernicke's area, is an example.

Commissural fibers cross the midline and connect matching areas of the right and left hemispheres. The largest is the corpus callosum, a thick, arched band of white matter at the bottom of the longitudinal fissure. On a midsagittal section it is the C-shaped white band above the diencephalon, and it forms the roof of the lateral ventricles. The anterior commissure and posterior commissure are smaller commissural bundles. Hanging below the corpus callosum is the septum pellucidum, a thin sheet that separates the two lateral ventricles. The fornix runs along its lower edge.

Projection fibers connect the cortex with the lower brain and spinal cord. Some carry signals down from the cortex, and some carry signals up to it. Just below the cortex they spread out in a fan called the corona radiata. Deeper down they gather into one compact band, the internal capsule. The internal capsule passes between the thalamus and caudate nucleus on its medial side and the lentiform nucleus (putamen and globus pallidus) on its lateral side. Below that, the descending fibers continue into the midbrain as the crus cerebri.

Basal ganglia

You should be able to: Identify the caudate nucleus, putamen, and globus pallidus and locate the functionally grouped substantia nigra and subthalamic nucleus.

The basal nuclei, also called the basal ganglia, are paired masses of gray matter buried deep in the cerebral white matter of each hemisphere. They lie beside the lateral ventricle and the thalamus. Clusters of cell bodies in the central nervous system are usually called nuclei, so many texts use basal nuclei. Basal ganglia is the older name and is still widely used. The basal nuclei work with the motor cortex. They help start wanted movements, hold back unwanted ones, and set muscle tone.

The caudate nucleus is long and curved, with a head, a body, and a tail. Its large head bulges into the lateral wall of the front part (anterior horn) of the lateral ventricle. The body and tail follow the curve of the ventricle backward and then down into the temporal lobe.

The putamen and globus pallidus together form a lens-shaped mass called the lentiform nucleus. It sits lateral to the internal capsule. The putamen is the outer, lateral part. The globus pallidus is the inner, medial part, and it looks paler. The caudate nucleus and putamen together are called the striatum, because bands of gray matter cross the internal capsule between them and give the area a striped look.

Two more nuclei sit outside the cerebrum but work in the same movement circuits, so they are grouped with the basal nuclei by function. The substantia nigra is a band of dark, pigmented neurons in the midbrain, just behind the crus cerebri. Loss of these neurons is the change seen in Parkinson disease. The subthalamic nucleus is a small nucleus in the diencephalon, below the thalamus and just above the substantia nigra.

Diencephalon

You should be able to: Identify the thalamus with the massa intermedia, the hypothalamus, the epithalamus with the pineal gland, and the third ventricle they enclose.

The diencephalon lies between the cerebrum and the midbrain. The cerebral hemispheres cover it, so you see it best on a midsagittal section. It has three parts, the thalamus, the hypothalamus, and the epithalamus, and together they surround the third ventricle.

The thalamus is a pair of egg-shaped masses of gray matter, one on each side of the third ventricle. It makes up about 80 percent of the diencephalon. The thalamus is made of many nuclei, and it relays most sensory input on its way to the cerebral cortex. The massa intermedia (interthalamic adhesion) is a small bridge of gray matter that crosses the third ventricle and joins the two thalami. On a midsagittal section it shows up as a small cut oval in the middle of the thalamus. Some people do not have one.

The hypothalamus lies below the thalamus. A shallow groove on the wall of the third ventricle, the hypothalamic sulcus, marks the border between them. The hypothalamus forms the floor and the lower walls of the third ventricle. It is the main control center for homeostasis. It controls the pituitary gland and the autonomic nervous system and helps regulate body temperature, hunger, thirst, and sleep and wake cycles. On the inferior surface of the brain, three landmarks belong to this area. The optic chiasm sits at its front, where the optic nerves meet and some of their fibers cross before continuing as the optic tracts. The infundibulum is the stalk behind the chiasm that connects the hypothalamus to the pituitary gland. The mammillary bodies are two small round bumps behind the infundibulum.

The epithalamus is the small, posterior, upper part of the diencephalon. It forms part of the roof of the third ventricle. Its main structure is the pineal gland, a small pine-cone-shaped endocrine gland that projects backward from the roof of the third ventricle, above the midbrain. The epithalamus also holds the habenular nuclei.

The third ventricle is the narrow, midline cavity enclosed by these parts. The thalami form its upper side walls, the hypothalamus forms its floor and lower walls, and the epithalamus forms the back of its roof. In front, it connects to each lateral ventricle through an interventricular foramen. Behind, it drains into the cerebral aqueduct of the midbrain.

Cerebellum

You should be able to: Identify the cerebellar hemispheres, vermis, folia, arbor vitae, deep nuclei, and the three cerebellar peduncles with their brainstem connections.

The cerebellum is the second largest part of the brain. It sits in the posterior cranial fossa, below the occipital lobes and behind the pons and medulla. The transverse fissure separates it from the cerebrum above. The fourth ventricle lies between the cerebellum and the brainstem. The cerebellum coordinates movement, posture, and balance, adjusting the timing and accuracy of movements without our awareness.

The cerebellum has two cerebellar hemispheres, joined in the midline by a narrow strip called the vermis. Its surface is folded into folia, thin, parallel, leaf-like folds that are much finer than the gyri of the cerebrum. A deep groove called the primary fissure divides the cerebellum into an anterior lobe and a posterior lobe, and a small flocculonodular lobe sits on its underside.

Inside, the cerebellum follows the same plan as the cerebrum. The cerebellar cortex is the outer layer of gray matter. Under it, the white matter branches out into every folium. On a midsagittal cut through the vermis this white matter looks like a branching tree, so it is called the arbor vitae, Latin for tree of life. Buried in the white matter are four pairs of deep cerebellar nuclei. From lateral to medial they are the dentate nucleus (the largest), the emboliform nucleus, the globose nucleus, and the fastigial nucleus. Most signals leaving the cerebellum start in these nuclei.

Three pairs of cerebellar peduncles, thick stalks of white matter, attach the cerebellum to the brainstem. Every fiber entering or leaving the cerebellum passes through one of them. Each peduncle joins a different part of the brainstem.

Limbic system

You should be able to: Locate the cingulate gyrus, amygdala, and hippocampus as the limbic structures ringing the diencephalon.

The limbic system is a group of structures on the medial side of each hemisphere. Limbic comes from limbus, meaning border or ring. These structures form a ring around the diencephalon and the top of the brainstem. They work together in emotion and memory.

The cingulate gyrus is the gyrus that arches just above the corpus callosum on the medial surface. Below it, the sulcus of the corpus callosum separates it from the corpus callosum. Above it, the cingulate sulcus separates it from the rest of the frontal and parietal lobes. It helps connect emotion with behavior. On a midsagittal section it is the first gyrus above the corpus callosum.

The cingulate gyrus curves around the back of the corpus callosum and continues down into the temporal lobe as the parahippocampal gyrus, on the inferior and medial surface of the temporal lobe. The front end of the parahippocampal gyrus hooks back on itself to form the uncus. Together these gyri make the C-shaped ring that gives the system its name.

The hippocampus is a curved ridge of cortex rolled into the floor of the inferior horn of the lateral ventricle (also called the temporal horn), deep to the parahippocampal gyrus in the medial temporal lobe. Its name means seahorse, which describes its shape. It is needed to form new long-term memories. Its output travels in the fornix, an arching band of white matter that runs forward under the corpus callosum, along the lower edge of the septum pellucidum, and ends at the mammillary bodies of the hypothalamus.

The amygdala is an almond-shaped nucleus deep in the front of the medial temporal lobe, just in front of the hippocampus and deep to the uncus. It is involved in fear and other emotional responses, and it links emotion to memory.

Cerebral arterial supply

You should be able to: Trace the anterior and posterior circulations through the Circle of Willis and identify the anterior, middle, and posterior cerebral arteries, the communicating arteries, the internal carotid, vertebral, and basilar arteries, and the cerebellar branches.

The brain is supplied by two pairs of arteries. The two internal carotid arteries form the anterior circulation. The two vertebral arteries form the posterior circulation. The two systems join in a ring of arteries on the inferior surface of the brain, the cerebral arterial circle, also called the Circle of Willis. The ring surrounds the optic chiasm, the infundibulum, and the mammillary bodies.

Anterior circulation

Each internal carotid artery rises through the neck, enters the skull through the carotid canal of the temporal bone, and reaches the base of the brain beside the optic chiasm. There it divides into two branches. The anterior cerebral artery runs forward and medially into the longitudinal fissure and arches over the corpus callosum. It supplies the medial surface of the frontal and parietal lobes, including the leg and foot areas of the motor and somatosensory cortex. The anterior communicating artery is a short bridge between the right and left anterior cerebral arteries, in front of the optic chiasm. The middle cerebral artery is the largest branch of the internal carotid. It runs laterally into the lateral sulcus and supplies most of the lateral surface of the hemisphere. That territory includes the face and upper limb areas of the motor and somatosensory cortex, the auditory cortex, the insula, and Broca's and Wernicke's areas.

Posterior circulation

The vertebral arteries branch from the subclavian arteries and rise through the transverse foramina of the cervical vertebrae. They enter the skull through the foramen magnum and run up the front of the medulla. At the junction of the medulla and pons, the two vertebral arteries unite to form the basilar artery. The basilar artery runs up the front of the pons in the midline, giving off small pontine arteries to the pons. At the top of the pons it divides into the two posterior cerebral arteries. Each posterior cerebral artery curves around the midbrain to supply the occipital lobe, including the visual cortex, and the inferior surface of the temporal lobe. A posterior communicating artery on each side joins the internal carotid artery to the posterior cerebral artery. These two vessels link the anterior and posterior circulations.

Cerebellar and spinal branches

Three pairs of cerebellar arteries come from the posterior circulation. The posterior inferior cerebellar artery (PICA) is the largest branch of the vertebral artery and supplies the underside of the cerebellum and the lateral medulla. The anterior inferior cerebellar artery (AICA) branches from the lower basilar artery and supplies the front part of the underside of the cerebellum. The superior cerebellar artery branches from the top of the basilar artery, just below the posterior cerebral artery, and supplies the upper surface of the cerebellum. The vertebral arteries also give rise to the spinal arteries. A small branch from each vertebral artery joins its partner in the midline to form the single anterior spinal artery, which runs down the front of the spinal cord. The paired posterior spinal arteries arise from the vertebral arteries or from the posterior inferior cerebellar arteries and run down the back of the cord.

The ring itself is made of the anterior communicating artery, the first part of each anterior cerebral artery, the end of each internal carotid artery, the two posterior communicating arteries, and the first part of each posterior cerebral artery. The middle cerebral arteries branch off the ring and are not part of it. Trace the route from a vertebral artery into the ring and around to the front:

  1. Vertebral artery enters the skull through the foramen magnum and runs up the front of the medulla; gives off the posterior inferior cerebellar artery.
  2. Basilar artery formed where the two vertebral arteries unite; runs up the front of the pons, giving off the anterior inferior cerebellar arteries, pontine arteries, and superior cerebellar arteries.
  3. Posterior cerebral artery one of the two end branches of the basilar artery at the top of the pons.
  4. Posterior communicating artery runs forward from the posterior cerebral artery to the internal carotid artery.
  5. Internal carotid artery reaches the base of the brain beside the optic chiasm; gives off the middle cerebral artery laterally.
  6. Anterior cerebral artery runs forward and medially toward the longitudinal fissure.
  7. Anterior communicating artery crosses the midline to the anterior cerebral artery of the other side, closing the front of the ring.

Location names the deficit

Because each lobe and cortical area has its own job, the location of an injury predicts the loss. Damage to the occipital lobe affects vision. Damage to Broca's area makes speech slow and effortful, while understanding of language is largely spared. The artery involved predicts the pattern too. A stroke in the middle cerebral artery affects the face and arm on the opposite side more than the leg. A stroke in the anterior cerebral artery affects the opposite leg most. A clinician can often estimate where a stroke struck from the pattern of symptoms alone.

The projection fibers of the internal capsule carry most of the traffic between the cortex and the body through one narrow band. A small stroke there can cut a great deal of wiring at once, which is why such a small lesion can weaken an entire side of the body.

The Circle of Willis gives the brain a backup route. If one artery feeding the ring narrows slowly, such as one internal carotid artery, blood can sometimes reach its territory through the communicating arteries. This only helps for blockages in the vessels that feed the ring. A blockage in a cerebral artery beyond the ring has no backup route through the circle, and the tissue that artery feeds is at risk. In many people one of the communicating arteries is small or missing, which also limits the backup.

Brainstem regions

You should be able to: Locate the midbrain, pons, and medulla oblongata in order and describe how the brainstem connects the higher brain to the spinal cord.

The brainstem is the stalk of the brain. It sits below the diencephalon, in front of the cerebellum, and above the spinal cord. It has three regions. From top to bottom they are the midbrain, the pons, and the medulla oblongata.

The midbrain, also called the mesencephalon, is the short, narrow region just below the diencephalon. The pons is the rounded bulge below the midbrain. Its name means bridge. The medulla oblongata is the lowest region. It tapers down and becomes the spinal cord at the foramen magnum.

On the front of a brain model or sheep brain, look for the groove where the pons meets the medulla. This is the pontomedullary junction. It is a useful landmark, because three cranial nerves (VI, VII, and VIII) leave the brainstem along it.

The brainstem connects the higher brain to the spinal cord in three ways. First, every ascending tract carrying sensory signals up to the brain and every descending tract carrying motor commands down to the cord passes through it. Second, it holds the nuclei of cranial nerves III through X and XII. The nucleus of XI sits just below it, in the upper cervical spinal cord. Third, three pairs of cerebellar peduncles tie the cerebellum to it: the superior peduncles to the midbrain, the middle peduncles to the pons, and the inferior peduncles to the medulla.

Midbrain structures

You should be able to: Identify the tectum with superior and inferior colliculi, the cerebral peduncles, the substantia nigra, and the cerebral aqueduct on a midbrain specimen.

The midbrain surrounds a narrow channel, the cerebral aqueduct. The aqueduct carries cerebrospinal fluid from the third ventricle down to the fourth ventricle. On a cross section of the midbrain it is the small hole near the back. Use it to orient the specimen: everything behind the aqueduct is the roof, and everything in front of it is the floor and the stalks.

The roof of the midbrain is the tectum. It carries four rounded bumps, together called the corpora quadrigemina. The upper pair are the superior colliculi. They are reflex centers for vision, such as turning the eyes and head toward a moving object. The lower pair are the inferior colliculi. They are reflex centers and relay stations for hearing, such as turning the head toward a sudden sound.

The front of the midbrain is formed by the two cerebral peduncles. These thick stalks carry the descending motor tracts from the cerebrum down to the pons, medulla, and spinal cord. The most anterior part of each peduncle is a band of white matter called the crus cerebri. Between the aqueduct and the crura lies the tegmentum, the core of the midbrain, which holds cranial nerve nuclei and part of the reticular formation.

The substantia nigra is a dark band of gray matter between the crus cerebri and the tegmentum. Its neurons contain a dark pigment, which is why it looks black in a fresh cross section. It works with the basal nuclei to help control movement.

Two cranial nerves leave the midbrain. The oculomotor nerve (CN III) leaves the front, in the gap between the two cerebral peduncles. The trochlear nerve (CN IV) leaves the back, just below the inferior colliculi.

Pons structures

You should be able to: Identify the pons, its pontine nuclei, the middle cerebellar peduncles, and the fourth ventricle behind it.

The pons is the rounded bulge between the midbrain and the medulla. Its front surface is the basilar pons, a thick band of fibers that runs crosswise and curves back on each side toward the cerebellum. The back part of the pons is its tegmentum, which carries cranial nerve nuclei and part of the reticular formation.

Scattered through the basilar pons are the pontine nuclei. These clusters of gray matter receive motor information from the cerebral cortex. Their axons cross to the opposite side and enter the cerebellum. This relay is how the cerebrum keeps the cerebellum informed about planned movements.

The axons from the pontine nuclei leave the pons as the middle cerebellar peduncles. These are the largest of the three pairs of cerebellar peduncles. They are the thick stalks that attach the side of the pons to each cerebellar hemisphere. On a model, follow the crosswise fibers of the basilar pons around the side and into the cerebellum.

Behind the pons lies the fourth ventricle. It is a diamond-shaped space filled with cerebrospinal fluid. Its floor is the back of the pons and the upper medulla, and the cerebellum lies over its roof. The cerebral aqueduct opens into it from above.

The pons also holds nuclei that help adjust the breathing rhythm set by the medulla. The trigeminal nerve (CN V) leaves the side of the pons. The abducens (CN VI), facial (CN VII), and vestibulocochlear (CN VIII) nerves leave at the pontomedullary junction.

Medulla oblongata structures

You should be able to: Identify the pyramids, olives, and the decussation of the pyramids on the medulla and locate the vital cardiac, respiratory, and vasomotor centers.

The medulla oblongata is the lowest region of the brainstem. It runs from the pontomedullary junction down to the foramen magnum, where it becomes the spinal cord. Its upper half forms the lower part of the floor of the fourth ventricle. Its lower half surrounds a narrow channel that continues into the cord as the central canal.

The front of the medulla has two long ridges, one on each side of the midline. These are the pyramids. Each pyramid is made of the corticospinal tract, the descending motor pathway from the cerebral cortex to the spinal cord.

Near the bottom of the medulla, most corticospinal fibers cross the midline to the opposite side. This crossing is the decussation of the pyramids. On a model it shows as fibers that cross over and blur the groove between the two pyramids. Because of this crossing, the left cerebral hemisphere controls the muscles on the right side of the body, and the right hemisphere controls the left side.

Just lateral to each pyramid is an oval swelling, the olive (the medullary olive). It is formed by the inferior olivary nucleus underneath. This nucleus relays information to the cerebellum through the inferior cerebellar peduncle. On the front of the medulla, the hypoglossal nerve (CN XII) leaves in the groove between the pyramid and the olive. The glossopharyngeal (CN IX) and vagus (CN X) nerves leave in the groove behind the olive.

The medulla also holds three vital centers in its core. They are part of the reticular formation and are not visible on the surface. The cardiac center adjusts the rate and force of the heartbeat. The respiratory center sets the basic rhythm of breathing. The vasomotor center adjusts the diameter of blood vessels and so helps set blood pressure.

A small region with vital jobs

The medulla oblongata holds the cardiac center, respiratory center, and vasomotor center. Because these centers control the heartbeat and breathing, an injury to the medulla can be life-threatening in a way that an injury to a single area of cerebral cortex is not.

The decussation of the pyramids is the anatomical reason a stroke in the right cerebral hemisphere weakens the left side of the body. Most of the motor fibers have already crossed by the time they reach the spinal cord.

The reticular activating system links the brainstem to consciousness. Damage to this ascending network in the upper brainstem can cause a coma, even when the cerebral cortex itself is not injured. The substantia nigra links the brainstem to movement: loss of its pigmented neurons is the basis of Parkinson disease.

Cranial nerve nuclei distribution

You should be able to: Match cranial nerve nuclei III through XII to the brainstem region that houses them.

A cranial nerve nucleus is a cluster of neuron cell bodies inside the brain that gives rise to the motor fibers of a cranial nerve or receives its sensory fibers. Cranial nerves I (olfactory) and II (optic) connect to the forebrain, so they have no brainstem nucleus. The nuclei of cranial nerves III through X and XII are housed in the brainstem, and the nucleus of XI sits just below it in the upper cervical spinal cord.

The nuclei line up from top to bottom in the same order as the nerve numbers. The midbrain holds III and IV. The pons holds V, VI, VII, and VIII. The medulla holds IX, X, and XII. A nerve usually leaves the brainstem near the level of its nucleus, so the attachment point on a model tells you the region.

Three nerves need a closer look. The trigeminal nerve (CN V) is assigned to the pons, where its main motor and sensory nuclei sit, but its sensory nuclei stretch up into the midbrain and down through the medulla. The vestibulocochlear nerve (CN VIII) has its vestibular and cochlear nuclei at the junction of the pons and medulla, so it is listed with both. The accessory nerve (CN XI) is grouped with the medulla in the 2-2-4-4 rule below, but its motor nucleus sits in the upper cervical spinal cord. Its rootlets rise through the foramen magnum and then leave the skull with the vagus nerve.

Reticular formation

You should be able to: Locate the reticular formation as a net of gray matter through the brainstem core and distinguish its ascending reticular activating system from its descending component.

The reticular formation is a loose net of gray matter that runs through the core of the whole brainstem, from the medulla up through the pons and the midbrain. Its name comes from the Latin for net. It is made of small clusters of neurons scattered among bundles of white matter, so it has no sharp borders and no surface landmark.

To locate it, look at any brainstem cross section. The reticular formation fills the central core (the tegmentum), behind the large descending motor tracts and in front of the ventricle or aqueduct. Its neurons connect with the cranial nerve nuclei, the cerebellum, the thalamus, and the spinal cord.

The reticular formation has two components that run in opposite directions. The ascending reticular activating system, or RAS, sends fibers up through the thalamus to the cerebral cortex. It keeps the cortex awake and alert, holds attention, and filters out unimportant sensory input so the cortex is not flooded. RAS activity falls during sleep. Damage to it can cause a coma.

The descending component sends fibers down to the spinal cord. These fibers help set the resting tone of skeletal muscles and help keep posture. The cardiac, respiratory, and vasomotor centers of the medulla are also part of the reticular formation, so this descending output links the brainstem to heart rate, breathing, and blood vessel diameter.

Cranial meninges and dural reflections

You should be able to: Identify the dura, arachnoid, and pia mater and the dural reflections falx cerebri, falx cerebelli, and tentorium cerebelli, and trace dural venous sinus drainage from the superior sagittal sinus to the internal jugular vein.

Three connective tissue membranes, the meninges, wrap the brain. From outside to inside they are the dura mater, the arachnoid mater, and the pia mater. Dura mater means tough mother, and pia mater means tender mother. Arachnoid means spider-like, for the web of fine strands it sends down to the pia.

Around the brain, the dura has two layers. The periosteal layer is attached to the inner surface of the skull. The meningeal layer lies inside it. The two layers are fused together except in a few places where they separate. The spaces between them form the dural venous sinuses, channels that collect venous blood from the brain.

The arachnoid mater is a thin, delicate membrane just inside the dura. It does not dip into the grooves of the brain. Fine strands cross from it to the pia, through the CSF-filled space between them. The pia mater is a very thin membrane rich in small blood vessels. It clings to the surface of the brain and follows it into every sulcus.

Spinal meninges and meningeal spaces

You should be able to: Identify the spinal meninges, the denticulate ligaments and filum terminale, and distinguish the epidural, subdural, and subarachnoid spaces by location and contents.

The same three meninges continue down the vertebral canal around the spinal cord. The spinal dura mater is a single layer. It matches the meningeal layer of the cranial dura and is not attached to the vertebrae. It forms a tube, the dural sac, that ends at about the level of S2. The spinal arachnoid mater lines the dural sac. The spinal pia mater clings to the surface of the cord.

Two extensions of the pia mater hold the cord in place. The denticulate ligaments are tooth-shaped flaps of pia along each side of the cord, between the dorsal and ventral roots. They reach out through the arachnoid and attach to the dura, which keeps the cord centered in the dural sac. The filum terminale is a thin strand of pia that continues down from the bottom tip of the cord. It passes through the lumbar cistern, picks up a covering of dura at the end of the dural sac, and anchors to the coccyx.

The meningeal spaces

The epidural space lies between the dura and the bone. In the vertebral canal it is a real space, filled with fat and veins, and it is where an epidural injection goes. In the skull the periosteal dura is attached to the bone, so the cranial epidural space is only a potential space. It opens only if something, such as blood, separates the dura from the skull.

The subdural space lies between the dura and the arachnoid. Normally the two membranes touch, so it is a potential space, holding only a thin film of fluid. The subarachnoid space lies between the arachnoid and the pia. It is a real space. It holds cerebrospinal fluid, the web-like strands of the arachnoid, and the larger blood vessels that run over the surface of the brain and cord.

The spinal cord is shorter than the vertebral column. In an adult it ends at about the level of the disc between L1 and L2. The tapered, cone-shaped end of the cord is the conus medullaris.

The nerve roots for the lumbar, sacral, and coccygeal spinal nerves leave the cord above that level. They have to travel down the vertebral canal before they reach their exit openings. This bundle of long roots below the conus medullaris is the cauda equina, which means horse's tail.

The dural sac and the subarachnoid space continue down to about S2. Below the conus medullaris the subarachnoid space is wide and holds no spinal cord, only CSF, the roots of the cauda equina, and the filum terminale. This enlarged space is the lumbar cistern.

The spinal cord stops at the conus medullaris near the L1 to L2 disc, yet the subarachnoid space runs lower, as the lumbar cistern. This difference in length is the basis of the lumbar puncture. A needle placed between the L3 and L4 or L4 and L5 spines reaches a pool of CSF where only the floating cauda equina lies in its path.

Knowing the layers in order is what makes the tap safe. The needle crosses skin, subcutaneous tissue, three ligaments, the epidural space, and then the dura and arachnoid before CSF appears. The firm ligamentum flavum giving way is a landmark the clinician feels.

The CSF drawn this way is tested for signs of meningitis or bleeding. The named spaces also tell bleeds apart: an epidural hematoma lies between the skull and the dura, a subdural hematoma lies just beneath the dura, and a subarachnoid hemorrhage mixes into the CSF itself.

Ventricular system

You should be able to: Identify the lateral, third, and fourth ventricles and the central canal and describe how the interventricular foramina, cerebral aqueduct, and fourth ventricle apertures connect them.

The ventricles are connected, fluid-filled spaces inside the brain. There are four: two lateral ventricles, the third ventricle, and the fourth ventricle. They are lined by ependymal cells, a type of glial cell, and filled with cerebrospinal fluid. Below the fourth ventricle the same system continues as the central canal of the spinal cord.

The two lateral ventricles are the largest. There is one in each cerebral hemisphere. Each is C-shaped, curving from the frontal lobe back and then down into the temporal lobe. In the midline the two lateral ventricles are separated by a thin sheet called the septum pellucidum.

The third ventricle is a narrow slit in the midline of the diencephalon, between the right and left halves of the thalamus. Each lateral ventricle opens into it through a small hole, the interventricular foramen (foramen of Monro). There is one foramen on each side.

The third ventricle drains down through the cerebral aqueduct (aqueduct of Sylvius), the narrow channel through the midbrain. The aqueduct opens into the fourth ventricle, which lies behind the pons and upper medulla and in front of the cerebellum.

The fourth ventricle has two kinds of exits. Three apertures in its roof and sides open into the subarachnoid space around the brain: one median aperture (foramen of Magendie) in the midline and two lateral apertures (foramina of Luschka), one on each side. Separately, the bottom of the fourth ventricle narrows directly into the central canal, which runs down the center of the spinal cord. The apertures lead only to the subarachnoid space, and the central canal opens only from the fourth ventricle itself.

CSF production and circulation

You should be able to: Trace CSF from production at the choroid plexus through the ventricles and subarachnoid space to reabsorption at the arachnoid granulations.

Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) is a clear, watery fluid that fills the ventricles and central canal and surrounds the brain and spinal cord in the subarachnoid space. It floats the brain, which lowers the brain's effective weight. It cushions the brain and cord against jolts. It also keeps a stable chemical environment around the neurons.

CSF is made by the choroid plexus. A choroid plexus is a tuft of capillaries covered by ependymal cells that hangs into a ventricle. The ependymal cells make CSF from the blood plasma in the capillaries. There is choroid plexus in all four ventricles, and the largest amounts are in the lateral ventricles. About 150 mL of CSF surrounds the CNS at any one time, and about 500 mL is made each day, so the fluid is replaced several times a day.

CSF moves one way through the ventricles, out into the subarachnoid space, and back into the blood. It is reabsorbed at the arachnoid granulations, small knobs of arachnoid mater that push through the dura into the dural venous sinuses, mainly the superior sagittal sinus. Blood-brain barrier structure

You should be able to: Name the three structural components of the blood-brain barrier the tight-junctioned capillary endothelium, the basement membrane, and the astrocyte end-feet.

The blood-brain barrier (BBB) controls what can pass from the blood into the brain tissue. It keeps out many substances, including many drugs and germs, while letting in what neurons need, such as glucose and oxygen. It is a structure built around the brain's capillaries, with three components.

The first and most important component is the capillary endothelium. The endothelial cells that line brain capillaries are joined by tight junctions. These seal the gaps between neighboring cells. Brain capillaries also lack the pores and wide clefts found in many capillaries elsewhere in the body. As a result, most substances can only cross by passing through the endothelial cells themselves.

The second component is the basement membrane, a thin layer of extracellular material that surrounds the endothelium. The third is the astrocyte end-feet. Astrocytes are star-shaped glial cells. Their processes end in flattened feet that wrap around the outside of the capillary, covering the basement membrane. The end-feet support the endothelial cells and help keep the tight junctions in place.

The barrier is missing in a few small areas around the ventricles, such as parts of the hypothalamus, where the brain needs to sample the blood directly.

Nervous system divisions

You should be able to: Outline the organization of the nervous system into CNS and PNS and its somatic, autonomic, and enteric motor divisions.

The nervous system is the body's fast control system. It senses changes, decides what they mean, and sends out commands. Anatomists divide it in two ways: by where the structures sit, and by what the motor neurons control.

The central nervous system (CNS) is the brain and the spinal cord. The brain sits in the cranial cavity. The spinal cord runs down the vertebral canal and links the brain to the body. The CNS is where information is processed and where commands are issued.

The peripheral nervous system (PNS) is every neural structure outside the brain and spinal cord. It includes the nerves, the ganglia, and the sensory receptors. There are 12 pairs of cranial nerves, which attach to the brain, and 31 pairs of spinal nerves, which attach to the spinal cord. The PNS carries information between the CNS and every other part of the body.

The PNS has a sensory side and a motor side. The sensory (afferent) division carries signals from receptors toward the CNS. The motor (efferent) division carries commands away from the CNS to the effectors, the muscles and glands that respond.

The motor side is split by the effector it controls. The somatic nervous system sends commands to skeletal muscle, and you control it voluntarily. The autonomic nervous system (ANS) sends commands to smooth muscle, cardiac muscle, and glands, and it works without conscious control. The ANS has two divisions: the sympathetic division for fight or flight, and the parasympathetic division for rest and digest. Many organs receive both.

The enteric nervous system (ENS) is a network of roughly 100 million neurons in the wall of the digestive tract. It controls the smooth muscle and glands of the gut and can run on its own, though the ANS connects to it and adjusts it. Some textbooks count it as part of the ANS.

Neuron structure

You should be able to: Identify the cell body, dendrites, axon, axon hillock, Nissl bodies, and axon terminals of a neuron and state the direction a signal travels through them.

A neuron is the nerve cell that carries signals. Mature neurons are excitable, they can live for a whole lifetime, and they generally do not divide. Every neuron has a cell body and two kinds of processes: dendrites and an axon.

The cell body (also called the soma or perikaryon) holds the nucleus, which has a large, dark nucleolus, and most of the cytoplasm and organelles. It is the metabolic center of the neuron and makes the proteins the whole cell needs. Scattered through its cytoplasm are Nissl bodies, clusters of rough endoplasmic reticulum and free ribosomes where those proteins are made. Nissl bodies stain dark on a slide and appear in the cell body and the dendrites. Neurofibrils, bundles of cytoskeleton filaments, run through the cell and give it its shape.

Dendrites are the short, heavily branched processes that receive incoming signals. They carry those signals toward the cell body. A multipolar neuron, the kind you see on the lab slide and the neuron model, has many dendrites.

The axon hillock is the cone-shaped region where the cell body narrows into the axon. It has no Nissl bodies, so it stains paler than the rest of the cell body. That pale cone is how you find the axon on a slide and tell it apart from a dendrite. Nerve impulses begin at the trigger zone, where the axon hillock meets the first part of the axon.

The axon is the single long process that carries the impulse away from the cell body. Its cytoplasm is the axoplasm and its plasma membrane is the axolemma. An axon can give off side branches called axon collaterals. At its far end the axon divides into many fine branches, the axon terminals, and the tip of each terminal swells into a synaptic end bulb that contacts the next cell.

  2. Cell body collects the signals arriving from the dendrites
  3. Axon hillock the impulse starts at the trigger zone where the hillock joins the axon
  4. Axon carries the impulse away from the cell body
  5. Axon terminals branch at the end of the axon and end in synaptic end bulbs that pass the signal to the next cell

Synapse structures

You should be able to: Identify the components of a chemical synapse including synaptic end bulbs, synaptic vesicles, and synaptic cleft, and name the three synapse types by the site they contact.

A synapse is the junction where a neuron passes its signal to another cell. The cell that sends the signal is the presynaptic neuron. The cell that receives it is the postsynaptic cell, which can be another neuron, a muscle fiber, or a gland cell. The synapse between a motor neuron and a skeletal muscle fiber is the neuromuscular junction.

At a chemical synapse the two cells do not touch. A narrow gap, the synaptic cleft, separates them. It is only about 20 to 50 nanometers wide. The signal crosses this gap as a chemical, a neurotransmitter. The other kind of synapse, an electrical synapse, joins two cells directly through gap junctions and is much less common.

On the sending side, each axon terminal ends in a swelling called a synaptic end bulb. Your lab list calls it the synaptic bulb (terminal end). Inside the end bulb are many synaptic vesicles, tiny membrane sacs that store neurotransmitter. The membrane of the end bulb that faces the cleft is the presynaptic membrane. Across the cleft, the postsynaptic membrane holds the receptors that the neurotransmitter binds to.

Only the presynaptic side has vesicles, so a chemical synapse passes its signal in one direction only.

  1. Synaptic end bulb the nerve impulse arrives at the end of the axon terminal
  2. Synaptic vesicles fuse with the presynaptic membrane and release neurotransmitter
  3. Synaptic cleft the neurotransmitter diffuses across the gap
  4. Postsynaptic membrane the neurotransmitter binds receptors, and the signal passes to the next cell

Three synapse types, named for the site the axon contacts

Each name starts with "axo" for the presynaptic axon and ends with the part of the next neuron it touches. An axodendritic synapse is an axon ending on a dendrite, and it is the most common type. An axosomatic synapse is an axon ending on a cell body (soma). An axoaxonic synapse is an axon ending on another axon.

Neuron classification

You should be able to: Classify neurons structurally as multipolar, bipolar, or unipolar and functionally as sensory, motor, or interneuron with an example of each.

Neurons are classified in two ways. The structural classification counts how many processes leave the cell body. The functional classification asks which direction the neuron carries its signal, toward the CNS or away from it.

Structural classification, by number of processes

A multipolar neuron has many dendrites and one axon. It is the most common type in the body. Motor neurons and interneurons are multipolar, and so is the neuron on your lab slide. Two multipolar neurons you will see on slides are the pyramidal cells of the cerebral cortex and the Purkinje cells of the cerebellum.

A bipolar neuron has one dendrite and one axon, leaving from opposite ends of the cell body. Bipolar neurons are rare. They are found in special sense organs: the retina of the eye, the olfactory epithelium of the nose, and the inner ear.

A unipolar neuron has one short process that leaves the cell body and then splits like the letter T into two branches. Some textbooks call it pseudounipolar. The peripheral branch starts at a receptor, and the central branch enters the CNS. Most sensory neurons are unipolar, and their cell bodies sit in the dorsal root ganglia and the cranial nerve ganglia.

Neuroglia identification

You should be able to: Name the four CNS glia and two PNS glia and describe the support role each one performs.

Neuroglia, or glial cells, are the support cells of nervous tissue. They are smaller than neurons and very numerous, though textbooks differ on how many glia there are for each neuron. Unlike mature neurons, they can still divide. In the CNS they make up about half the volume of the tissue. On a slide, the many small dark nuclei around the large neuron cell bodies belong to glia.

There are six types. Four are in the CNS and two are in the PNS.

The four glia of the CNS

Astrocytes are star-shaped cells and the most numerous glia in the CNS. Their processes wrap around neurons and capillaries. They hold neurons in place, help form the blood-brain barrier, and keep the fluid around neurons at the right chemical balance. In the embryo they guide growing neurons, and after an injury they fill the damaged area with scar tissue.

Oligodendrocytes have a few long processes. Each process wraps around a segment of an axon to form myelin, so one oligodendrocyte myelinates parts of several different axons. They are the myelin-forming cells of the CNS.

Microglia are small cells that act as phagocytes. They engulf microbes and clear away debris from dead and damaged cells. They are the CNS's own immune defense.

Ependymal cells are cuboidal to columnar cells, many with cilia, that line the ventricles of the brain and the central canal of the spinal cord. In the choroid plexuses they help produce cerebrospinal fluid, and their cilia help it circulate.

The two glia of the PNS

Schwann cells wrap around PNS axons. Each Schwann cell forms the myelin around one segment of a single axon. Schwann cells also enclose unmyelinated PNS axons, and they guide a damaged axon as it regrows.

Satellite cells are flat cells that surround the cell bodies of neurons in ganglia. They support those cell bodies and regulate the exchange of materials between the cell bodies and the fluid around them.

Myelination and regeneration

You should be able to: Describe the myelin sheath, nodes of Ranvier, and neurolemma and contrast PNS and CNS myelination by the glial cell involved and by regeneration potential.

The myelin sheath is a covering made of many layers of a glial cell's plasma membrane, wrapped tightly around an axon. Because it is mostly lipid with some protein, it looks white. It insulates the axon and makes the nerve impulse travel much faster. An axon with this covering is a myelinated axon.

Myelin is laid down in segments along the axon. The short gaps between neighboring segments are the nodes of Ranvier, where the axolemma is left bare. The myelin-covered stretch between two nodes is an internode. In a myelinated axon the impulse jumps from node to node. This fast form of conduction is called saltatory conduction.

The neurolemma is the outermost layer of a Schwann cell. It holds the Schwann cell's nucleus and most of its cytoplasm, and it sits outside the myelin layers. Only Schwann cells have one, so the neurolemma is found only around PNS axons.

PNS and CNS myelination compared

In the PNS, the myelin is made by Schwann cells. Each Schwann cell wraps one segment of one axon, so a long axon needs a row of Schwann cells along its length. In the CNS, the myelin is made by oligodendrocytes. One oligodendrocyte sends processes to several axons and myelinates a segment of each. Oligodendrocytes have no neurolemma.

That difference decides whether a cut axon can regrow. When a PNS axon is cut, the part beyond the cut breaks down. If the cell body is intact, the Schwann cells line up and their neurolemma forms a regeneration tube that guides the regrowing axon back toward its target. In the CNS there is no neurolemma to guide regrowth. Oligodendrocytes also release substances that block regrowth, and astrocytes fill the area with scar tissue. Regeneration in the CNS is very limited.

Myelination increases from birth to maturity, so an infant's nerve conduction is slower than an adult's.

When the support cells fail

Mature neurons generally do not divide, so tumors of nervous tissue rarely come from neurons. Most come from the glia, which can still divide. A glioma is a tumor of glial cells, and it is the most common primary malignant brain tumor.

Myelin matters as much as the axon it wraps. In multiple sclerosis, the immune system destroys myelin around CNS axons, and signals slow down or fail. The presence of the neurolemma on Schwann cells, and its absence on oligodendrocytes, is why a cut nerve in the arm can regrow while damage in the brain or spinal cord usually cannot be repaired.

The cell body keeps the whole neuron alive. The axon depends on the cell body for its proteins, so the part of an axon cut off from its cell body dies. An injury close to the cell body is more likely to kill the whole neuron than an injury far out along the axon.

CNS and PNS tissue terms

You should be able to: Define nucleus, ganglion, tract, and nerve and state whether each names a cluster of cell bodies or a bundle of axons in the CNS or PNS.

Four terms name groups of neurons. Two of them name clusters of neuron cell bodies, and two name bundles of axons. Which term you use depends on whether the group sits inside the CNS or out in the PNS.

A nucleus is a cluster of neuron cell bodies inside the CNS. The cranial nerve nuclei in the brainstem and the basal nuclei deep in the cerebrum are examples. Nuclei are made of gray matter. This meaning is different from the nucleus inside a single cell.

A ganglion (plural, ganglia) is a cluster of neuron cell bodies in the PNS. The dorsal root ganglion holds the cell bodies of sensory neurons, and the sympathetic trunk ganglia hold the cell bodies of autonomic neurons. Satellite cells surround the cell bodies in a ganglion. The basal nuclei of the brain are also called the basal ganglia, an older name that breaks this rule, since they sit in the CNS.

A tract is a bundle of axons inside the CNS. The corticospinal tract and the spinothalamic tract are examples. Tracts make up the white matter of the brain and spinal cord.

A nerve is a bundle of axons in the PNS, held together by connective tissue and supplied by blood vessels. The sciatic nerve and the vagus nerve are examples. A nerve holds axons but no cell bodies. The cell bodies of those axons sit in the CNS or in ganglia.

Gray and white matter

You should be able to: Distinguish gray matter from white matter by composition and locate each in the brain and the spinal cord.

Nervous tissue in the CNS sorts into two kinds by its look. Gray matter is made of neuron cell bodies, dendrites, unmyelinated axons, axon terminals, and neuroglia. It is where signals are received and processed. White matter is made mostly of myelinated axons, with the glia that myelinate them. It carries signals from one region to another. White matter looks white because myelin is rich in lipid.

In the spinal cord

In the spinal cord, gray matter is on the inside and white matter is on the outside. On a cross section, the gray matter forms a shape like a butterfly or the letter H. Its parts are the posterior (dorsal) horns, the anterior (ventral) horns, and, in the thoracic and upper lumbar cord (T1 to L2), the lateral horns. The central canal runs through the middle of the gray matter. The white matter around it is divided into posterior, lateral, and anterior columns, and these columns hold the tracts.

In the brain

In the cerebrum, the arrangement is reversed. A thin outer layer of gray matter, the cerebral cortex, covers the surface, and its large neurons include the pyramidal cells. Beneath the cortex is a large mass of white matter, including the corpus callosum. Deep inside the brain, surrounded by white matter, sit islands of gray matter called deep nuclei, such as the basal nuclei of the cerebrum and the thalamus of the diencephalon.

The cerebellum has the same plan. Its outer layer, the cerebellar cortex, is gray matter and contains the Purkinje cells. Its inner white matter branches like a tree and is called the arbor vitae. In the brainstem, nuclei of gray matter are scattered among the white matter tracts.

Reflex arc components

You should be able to: Name the five components of a reflex arc in order from receptor to effector and trace a signal through them.

A reflex is a fast, automatic, predictable response to a stimulus. The pathway a reflex follows is the reflex arc. A reflex is fast because the arc is short and fixed. When the integration center is in the spinal cord, it is a spinal reflex. When it is in the brainstem, it is a cranial reflex. Either way, no conscious decision is needed, though the brain may become aware of the response afterward.

Every reflex arc has the same five components, in the same order. The signal comes in on the sensory side, is processed in the CNS, and goes out on the motor side.

  1. Receptor detects the stimulus; it is the ending of a sensory neuron or a receptor cell next to one
  2. Sensory neuron carries the signal to the CNS; in a spinal reflex, its cell body sits in a dorsal root ganglion, and its axon enters the spinal cord through the dorsal root
  3. Integration center one or more synapses in the gray matter of the spinal cord or brainstem, where the signal passes to the next neuron
  4. Motor neuron carries the command away from the CNS; in a somatic spinal reflex, its cell body sits in the anterior horn, and its axon leaves through the ventral root
  5. Effector the muscle or gland that carries out the response

Trace a signal: the patellar reflex

The patellar (knee-jerk) reflex is the classic example. A tap on the patellar ligament, just below the kneecap, stretches the quadriceps femoris.

  1. Receptor muscle spindles in the quadriceps femoris detect the stretch
  2. Sensory neuron carries the signal through the femoral nerve and dorsal roots into the spinal cord at L2 to L4
  3. Integration center the sensory neuron synapses directly on a motor neuron in the anterior horn
  4. Motor neuron carries the command out through the ventral root and the femoral nerve
  5. Effector the quadriceps femoris contracts, and the leg extends at the knee

Kinds of receptors

Receptors are named for the stimulus they detect.

  • Mechanoreceptors: touch, pressure, and stretch
  • Thermoreceptors: temperature
  • Photoreceptors: light
  • Chemoreceptors: chemicals
  • Nociceptors: pain

Memory tipThe arc runs in, through, and out: Receptor, Sensory neuron, Integration center, Motor neuron, Effector. The sensory neuron enters by the dorsal root, and the motor neuron leaves by the ventral root.

Types of reflexes

You should be able to: Compare somatic versus autonomic, monosynaptic versus polysynaptic, and innate versus learned reflexes and give an example of each.

Reflexes are sorted in three ways: by the effector, by the number of synapses in the integration center, and by whether the reflex is inborn or learned. Each pair answers a different question, so one reflex fits into all three. The patellar reflex, for example, is somatic, monosynaptic, and innate.

Somatic versus autonomic

A somatic reflex has skeletal muscle as its effector. The patellar reflex is a somatic reflex. An autonomic reflex, also called a visceral reflex, has smooth muscle, cardiac muscle, or a gland as its effector. In the pupillary light reflex, bright light makes the pupils constrict. The effector is the smooth muscle of the iris, and the integration center is in the midbrain.

Monosynaptic versus polysynaptic

In a monosynaptic reflex, the sensory neuron synapses directly on the motor neuron, so there is only one synapse in the integration center. The stretch reflex, such as the patellar reflex, is monosynaptic. In a polysynaptic reflex, one or more interneurons sit between the sensory and motor neurons, so there are two or more synapses. The withdrawal reflex, pulling your hand away from a hot stove, is polysynaptic. A monosynaptic reflex is the faster of the two, because each synapse adds a short delay.

Innate versus learned

An innate reflex is present from birth and does not have to be learned. Blinking when something touches the eye is an innate reflex. A learned reflex, also called an acquired reflex, is built through practice and repetition until it runs automatically. An experienced driver braking when the car ahead stops suddenly, or catching a suddenly dropped object, are learned reflexes.

Neuronal pools and pathways

You should be able to: Describe convergence and divergence circuits and distinguish ascending pathways, descending pathways, and decussation.

Neurons in the CNS work in groups. A neuronal pool is a group of interconnected neurons that process the same kind of information together. A pool can hold thousands of neurons. The pattern of connections inside and between pools is called a neural circuit, and the pattern shapes how a signal is handled.

Convergence and divergence

In a convergence circuit, several presynaptic neurons synapse on one postsynaptic neuron. The one neuron can then combine input from many sources. A motor neuron in the anterior horn of the spinal cord is an example. It receives input from several regions of the brain and from sensory neurons, and its single output goes to a skeletal muscle.

In a divergence circuit, one presynaptic neuron branches and synapses on many postsynaptic neurons. One signal spreads out to reach many cells. A single motor neuron in the brain, for example, can stimulate many motor neurons in the spinal cord, which in turn reach many muscle fibers.

Signals can also travel in series or in parallel. In serial processing, the signal passes along a single chain of neurons, as in a reflex arc. In parallel processing, the same signal travels along several pathways at the same time.

Ascending pathways, descending pathways, and decussation

Long-distance signals travel through the CNS in tracts that run in the white matter. A tract's name usually tells you where it starts and where it ends. The spinothalamic tract runs from the spinal cord to the thalamus.

Ascending pathways carry sensory information up the spinal cord to the brain. The spinothalamic tract, which carries pain and temperature, is an ascending pathway. Descending pathways carry motor commands down from the brain to the spinal cord. The corticospinal tract, which runs from the cerebral cortex to the spinal cord and controls voluntary movement, is a descending pathway.

Decussation is the crossing of a pathway from one side of the CNS to the other. The main sensory and motor pathways cross, so each side of the brain senses and controls the opposite side of the body. Most corticospinal axons cross in the medulla oblongata, at the pyramidal decussation. Spinothalamic axons cross in the spinal cord, close to where the sensory fibers enter.

Reading the wiring in the clinic

A tap below the kneecap triggers the patellar reflex, and a clinician watches the leg kick. That one test checks the whole reflex arc: the receptor, the sensory and motor neurons, and the segment of spinal cord in between. A missing reflex points to a break somewhere along that pathway.

Because the main sensory and motor pathways decussate, the right side of the brain senses and controls the left side of the body. This is why a stroke in one cerebral hemisphere causes weakness on the opposite side of the body.

On an MRI scan, gray matter and white matter show up as different shades because myelin is rich in lipid. That contrast lets a radiologist see the cortex, the deep nuclei, and the white matter tracts, and spot damage such as the patches of lost myelin in multiple sclerosis.

Spinal cord external anatomy

You should be able to: Identify the cervical and lumbar enlargements, conus medullaris, cauda equina, filum terminale, anterior median fissure, and posterior median sulcus.

The spinal cord is the part of the central nervous system that sits inside the vertebral canal. It is continuous with the medulla oblongata at the foramen magnum. In an adult it is about 42 to 45 cm (16 to 18 inches) long. It carries signals between the brain and the body, and it is the center for spinal reflexes.

The cord is shorter than the vertebral column. The cord stops growing in length at about 4 to 5 years of age, while the vertebral column keeps growing. So in an adult the cord ends near the disc between L1 and L2. Below that level the vertebral canal holds only nerve roots and a thin anchoring thread.

The two enlargements

The width of the cord changes along its length. It is widest in the cervical region and thinner through the thoracic region. It swells in two places, where the nerves to the limbs leave. A limb has many muscles and a large area of skin, so these segments need more neurons.

  • The cervical enlargement is in the lower neck, from about C4 to T1. The nerves to the upper limbs (the brachial plexus) leave here.
  • The lumbar enlargement sits behind the lower thoracic vertebrae, about T9 to T12. Texts give slightly different levels, and some place it a little lower, near T11 to L1. The nerves to the lower limbs (the lumbar and sacral plexuses) leave here.

Where the cord ends

Below the lumbar enlargement the cord tapers to a point. This cone-shaped end is the conus medullaris. It sits near the disc between L1 and L2.

A thin strand of pia mater runs on from the tip of the conus medullaris. This strand is the filum terminale. It runs down the lower vertebral canal and anchors the cord to the coccyx.

The lumbar, sacral, and coccygeal nerve roots leave the cord high up, at or above the conus. They must travel down the vertebral canal to reach their exits lower in the column. Together these long roots look like a horse's tail, so they are called the cauda equina. The filum terminale runs down the middle of them.

The two grooves

Two grooves run the full length of the cord and split it into right and left halves. The anterior median fissure is on the front. It is deep and wide. The posterior median sulcus is on the back. It is shallow and narrow. On a model or a cross-section, the wide groove tells you which side is anterior.

Where the cord ends, and what that allows

The spinal cord ends at the conus medullaris near the L1 to L2 disc, but the vertebral column runs much lower. Below the conus, only the cauda equina and the filum terminale lie in the cerebrospinal fluid. That is why a lumbar puncture is done below the end of the cord, usually between L3 and L4 or between L4 and L5. A needle can draw cerebrospinal fluid there without reaching the cord itself.

The posterior root ganglion holds the sensory neuron cell bodies for one spinal nerve. The varicella zoster virus (the chickenpox virus) can stay dormant in these ganglia for decades. When it reactivates as shingles, the rash follows the strip of skin served by that one spinal nerve.

A complete spinal cord injury cuts the ascending tracts and descending tracts. These tracts carry the signals between the brain and the body below the injury. So sensation and movement are lost below the level of the injury, while function above it is spared.

Roots, rootlets, and root ganglion

You should be able to: Identify the rootlets, posterior and anterior roots, posterior root ganglion, and intervertebral foramen and state which root carries sensory versus motor axons.

Each segment of the spinal cord connects to a pair of spinal nerves, one on each side. On each side, two roots join the cord to the nerve: a posterior root and an anterior root.

Each root starts as a row of small, fan-shaped bundles of axons called rootlets. The posterior rootlets attach in a line along the back of the cord. The anterior rootlets leave in a line along the front of the cord. The rootlets of one segment merge to form one root.

The posterior root (dorsal root) is the sensory root. It carries sensory axons from receptors in the body into the cord. Along the posterior root is a swelling, the posterior root ganglion (dorsal root ganglion). It holds the cell bodies of the sensory neurons. These are unipolar neurons. One branch of the axon runs out to a receptor and the other runs into the cord.

The anterior root (ventral root) is the motor root. It carries motor axons out of the cord. Somatic motor axons go to skeletal muscle. In the T1 to L2 and S2 to S4 segments, the anterior root also carries autonomic axons that lead toward smooth muscle, cardiac muscle, and glands. The cell bodies of these motor neurons sit inside the cord, in the gray matter. That is why the anterior root has no ganglion.

Just outside the cord the two roots join to form the spinal nerve. It holds both sensory and motor axons, so every spinal nerve is a mixed nerve. The spinal nerve leaves the vertebral canal through the intervertebral foramen. This opening lies between two neighboring vertebrae, formed by the notch of the vertebra above and the notch of the vertebra below. The posterior root ganglion sits in or right next to this foramen.

Spinal cord internal anatomy

You should be able to: Identify the gray horns, central canal, commissures, and white columns on a cord cross-section and state the contents of the posterior, lateral, and anterior horns.

A cross-section of the spinal cord shows gray matter in the center and white matter around it. This is the reverse of the cerebrum, where gray matter forms the outer cortex. The gray matter is shaped like a butterfly or the letter H.

Gray matter holds neuron cell bodies, dendrites, unmyelinated axons, and neuroglia. White matter is mostly myelinated axons. The fatty myelin is what makes it look white.

The gray horns

Each half of the gray matter sticks out into projections called horns. Each horn holds a different kind of neuron.

  • The anterior column lies between the anterior horn and the anterior median fissure.

Nerve connective tissue wrappings

You should be able to: Identify the axon, fascicle, endoneurium, perineurium, and epineurium of a peripheral nerve and state the level each layer wraps.

A nerve is an organ of the peripheral nervous system. It is a bundle of many axons held together by connective tissue, with blood vessels running through it. Some of its axons are myelinated and some are not. A nerve is the PNS version of a tract in the CNS.

The smallest unit in a nerve is the single axon, also called a nerve fiber. In the PNS, myelinated axons are wrapped by Schwann cells. The small gaps between neighboring Schwann cells are the nodes of Ranvier.

  • Axons are grouped into bundles called fascicles. The perineurium wraps each fascicle.
  • The epineurium is a tough layer of dense irregular connective tissue around the whole nerve. It also fills the spaces between the fascicles and carries the nerve's blood vessels.

Because a nerve is wrapped in connective tissue, it has some protection and some ability to heal. The epineurium and perineurium hold a cut nerve's shape. A surgeon stitches these layers to line up the cut ends and repair the nerve.

The two rami serve different territories. The anterior rami supply the limbs and the front and sides of the trunk, so damage to the anterior rami or the plexuses they form shows up in a limb. A posterior ramus supplies only the deep back muscles and the skin over them, so damage there stays in the back.

Receptors are not spread evenly. The fingertips and lips are crowded with tactile corpuscles and Merkel discs, which is why those areas feel fine detail so well. The skin of the back has far fewer, so it senses touch only crudely.

Cranial nerves

You should be able to: Name the twelve cranial nerves in order, classify each as sensory, motor, or mixed, and match each to its primary function and skull foramen.

Twelve pairs of cranial nerves attach to the brain. They are part of the peripheral nervous system. They are numbered with Roman numerals, I to XII, from front to back in the order they attach to the brain. CN I and CN II attach to the forebrain. CN III to CN XII attach to the brainstem, except that most of CN XI comes from the upper cervical spinal cord.

A sentence helps with the order of the names: "On Old Olympus' Towering Tops A Finn And German Viewed Some Hops." The first letters stand for olfactory, optic, oculomotor, trochlear, trigeminal, abducens, facial, vestibulocochlear, glossopharyngeal, vagus, spinal accessory, and hypoglossal.

Sensory, motor, or mixed

Each cranial nerve is classed by the kind of axons it carries. A sensory nerve carries signals only into the brain. A motor nerve carries signals mainly out to muscles. A mixed nerve carries both.

  • Sensory: CN I, CN II, and CN VIII. These carry the special senses of smell, vision, hearing, and balance.
  • Motor: CN III, CN IV, CN VI, CN XI, and CN XII.
  • Mixed: CN V, CN VII, CN IX, and CN X.

Four cranial nerves also carry parasympathetic motor axons to smooth muscle and glands: CN III, CN VII, CN IX, and CN X. For the sensory, motor, or both pattern in order, use "Some Say Marry Money But My Brother Says Big Brains Matter Most."

Twelve nerves, twelve exams

Each cranial nerve has its own job, so a clinician can test the nerves one at a time. A weak or missing function points to the nerve that carries it. The nerve's attachment to the brain and its path through the skull then point to where the problem may be.

The openings group nerves together. The jugular foramen carries CN IX, X, and XI side by side, so a tumor or fracture there can affect all three at once. The result is a recognizable cluster of swallowing, voice, and shoulder signs.

Nerve plexuses

You should be able to: Name the cervical, brachial, lumbar, sacral, and coccygeal plexuses with the spinal nerves that form each and predict the body region affected if a given plexus is damaged.

The anterior rami of most spinal nerves do not run straight to their targets. The anterior rami of neighboring spinal nerves first join and branch again to form a network called a nerve plexus. Their axons are sorted and regrouped there, and then leave the plexus in named nerves.

Because of this regrouping, each nerve that leaves a plexus carries axons from several spinal nerves. So a body region gets axons from more than one spinal nerve. Damage to one spinal nerve weakens a region but does not usually wipe out all function there.

The thoracic region is the exception. The anterior rami of T2 to T12 do not form plexuses. They run between the ribs as the intercostal nerves (T12, below the last rib, is called the subcostal nerve). They supply the intercostal muscles, the muscles of the abdominal wall, and the skin of the chest and abdomen.

There are five plexuses. Each is named for its region and is formed by a set range of spinal nerves.

Why the network matters

The plexus design is a safety feature. Each limb region draws axons from several spinal nerves, so a single damaged spinal nerve root weakens an area but rarely paralyzes it completely.

Damage to the plexus itself is different. It can damage axons from several spinal nerves at once, so it can take out a whole region. A brachial plexus injury affects the upper limb, and a lumbar or sacral plexus injury affects the lower limb.

The phrenic nerve leaves the cervical plexus and is the only motor supply to the diaphragm. The phrase "C3, 4, 5 keeps the diaphragm alive" is a reminder that an injury to the cord at or above that level can stop breathing.

Autonomic two-neuron pathway

You should be able to: Contrast the somatic and autonomic motor systems and trace the two-neuron pathway through a preganglionic neuron, autonomic ganglion, and postganglionic neuron to an effector.

The motor side of the peripheral nervous system has two parts. The somatic nervous system controls skeletal muscle, and that control is usually voluntary. The autonomic nervous system (ANS) controls cardiac muscle, smooth muscle, and glands, and that control is involuntary. Both start in the central nervous system.

In the somatic system one neuron does the whole job. Its cell body sits in the anterior horn of the spinal cord or in a motor nucleus of the brainstem. Its myelinated axon runs all the way out to the skeletal muscle.

The autonomic system always uses two motor neurons in a row. The first is the preganglionic neuron. Its cell body is in the CNS, in a brainstem nucleus or in the lateral gray matter of the spinal cord. Its axon is thin and lightly myelinated. It leaves the CNS in a cranial nerve or an anterior root and ends in an autonomic ganglion.

An autonomic ganglion is a cluster of neuron cell bodies outside the CNS. Here the preganglionic axon synapses with the second neuron, the postganglionic neuron. The postganglionic cell body sits in the ganglion. Its unmyelinated axon runs from the ganglion out to the effector.

  1. Preganglionic neuron cell body in the brainstem or the lateral gray matter of the cord; its lightly myelinated axon leaves the CNS
  2. Autonomic ganglion the preganglionic axon synapses with the postganglionic neuron
  3. Postganglionic neuron cell body in the ganglion; its unmyelinated axon carries the signal the rest of the way
  4. Effector cardiac muscle, smooth muscle, or a gland receives the signal

The ANS also has sensory neurons. They carry signals from receptors in the organs and blood vessels back to the CNS. These signals help the CNS adjust the organs.

Memory tipPre means before the ganglion and post means after it. Do not mix up the two kinds of ganglia: a posterior root ganglion holds sensory cell bodies and has no synapses, while an autonomic ganglion holds motor cell bodies and is where the synapse happens.

Competency 193Lecture

Autonomic ganglia and divisions

You should be able to: Compare the sympathetic and parasympathetic divisions by CNS origin and ganglion location and identify the sympathetic trunk, prevertebral ganglia, terminal ganglia, and autonomic and enteric plexuses.

The ANS has two divisions, the sympathetic and the parasympathetic. Most organs get axons from both, and the two usually have opposite effects. The two divisions differ in where their preganglionic neurons start in the CNS and in where their ganglia sit.

The sympathetic division

The sympathetic division is the fight or flight division. It takes over during exercise, excitement, and emergencies. It is also called the thoracolumbar division, because its preganglionic cell bodies sit in the lateral horns of the T1 to L2 spinal segments. Its ganglia lie close to the spinal cord. So its preganglionic axons are short and its postganglionic axons are long.

The sympathetic trunk is a chain of ganglia on each side of the vertebral column, running from the base of the skull to the coccyx. Its ganglia are the sympathetic trunk ganglia, also called paravertebral or chain ganglia. Axons link one ganglion to the next. The trunk connects to the spinal nerves through the white and gray rami communicantes.

The prevertebral ganglia, also called collateral ganglia, lie in front of the vertebral column, close to the large branches of the abdominal aorta. They are the celiac, superior mesenteric, inferior mesenteric, aorticorenal, and renal ganglia. Preganglionic axons reach them by passing through the sympathetic trunk without synapsing, in nerves called splanchnic nerves.

Follow a sympathetic signal to a sweat gland in the skin.

  1. Lateral horn (T1 to L2) the preganglionic cell body sends out its axon
  2. Anterior root and spinal nerve the preganglionic axon leaves the cord
  3. White ramus communicans the preganglionic axon enters the sympathetic trunk
  4. Sympathetic trunk ganglion the preganglionic axon synapses with the postganglionic neuron
  5. Gray ramus communicans the postganglionic axon returns to the spinal nerve
  6. Effector the axon runs out in a ramus to a sweat gland, an arrector pili muscle, or a blood vessel

For the abdominal and pelvic organs, the path differs after the white ramus. The preganglionic axon passes through the trunk ganglion without synapsing and travels in a splanchnic nerve to a prevertebral ganglion. It synapses there. The postganglionic axon then follows the arteries to the organ.

The parasympathetic division

The parasympathetic division is the rest and digest division. It conserves energy and runs routine work such as digestion, urination, and defecation. It is also called the craniosacral division. Its preganglionic cell bodies sit in the brainstem nuclei of four cranial nerves (CN III, VII, IX, and X) and in the lateral gray matter of the S2 to S4 spinal segments.

Its ganglia are the terminal ganglia. They lie close to the organ or inside its wall (intramural ganglia). So its preganglionic axons are long and its postganglionic axons are short. The vagus nerve (CN X) carries preganglionic axons to terminal ganglia in the walls of the thoracic and abdominal organs. The pelvic splanchnic nerves from S2 to S4 carry them to the pelvic organs and the last part of the large intestine.

The enteric plexuses

The wall of the digestive tract has its own networks of neurons, the enteric plexuses. They run from the esophagus to the anus and form the enteric nervous system, which has about 100 million neurons. These networks can run the gut wall on their own, and the ANS adjusts their activity.

  • The myenteric plexus lies in the muscularis externa, between the circular and longitudinal muscle layers. It mainly controls the movement of the gut.
  • The submucosal plexus lies in the submucosa. It mainly controls the secretions of the gut.

Two divisions, opposite effects

The two divisions usually pull an organ in opposite directions. So the body can adjust an organ by easing off one division as well as by pushing the other. Heart rate, for example, rises when parasympathetic input is withdrawn, even before the sympathetic division speeds the heart up.

The vagus nerve, CN X, carries most of the parasympathetic supply to the chest and abdomen. Its long reach is why one nerve can slow the heart, narrow the airways, and speed digestion.

The enteric nervous system is independent enough to be called the brain of the gut. Its plexuses can keep the gut wall contracting in rhythm and its glands secreting even when the connections to the CNS are cut.
