What is this course about?

This course is about the structure of the human body, from cells all the way up to organ systems. You'll study it three ways: tissues under the microscope, models, and human donors.

Knowing a structure means you can say what it is, find where it is, and recognize what it looks like. Some competencies also ask for a basic function, and your Competency Packet tells you which ones.

What materials do I need?

You'll need:

  • your own lab coat, which stays in the lab all term
  • non-latex nitrile gloves (you can split a box with your team)
  • a laptop, tablet or phone with internet, for Canvas, the concept videos and Mastery OS
  • a way to save files as PDFs, for anything you turn in on Canvas

I give you printed copies of the module notes, the pre-work and the lab structure lists. Mastery OS, the concept videos, the Loops practice videos and the Digital Human Anatomy Atlas are all free.

You don't need to buy a textbook. If you'd like one, OpenStax Anatomy and Physiology (2nd edition) is free at openstax.org and covers everything in this course. Marieb, Brady and Mallatt's Human Anatomy (9th edition) also works, and used copies are easy to find. You don't need Mastering A&P for anything in this class, so please don't buy it.

The lab coat and gloves are your only costs. If paying for them is hard, talk to me or to the Basic Needs Center (Building 1400, Room 1409, 707-864-7261).

What is the digital device policy?

Phones, smart watches, AI glasses, tablets, laptops and recording devices aren't allowed in the lab at any time. Silence your devices and put them away out of sight, which means not in your pocket, on your lap or on the table. You also can't take photos, video, audio or digital notes in the lab. Use your digital materials outside the lab, at home or during open study hours.

We follow this rule because the people who donated their bodies can't give permission to be photographed or recorded.

Breaking this rule can get you removed from the course. You'll sign the digital device agreement in Canvas before your first lab. Recording any class without permission is also against college policy (Board Policy 5390).

How do I act around the donors?

Handle every donor and specimen with care. Keep the tissue covered and moist, and leave your station the way you found it. Please don't joke around, use nicknames or use careless language in the lab, and don't talk about the donors outside the lab or on social media.

Working with the donors is required. Half of the lab practical on Exams 3, 4 and 5 is on the donors, and there's no alternative assignment.

If you feel uneasy, come talk to me. Most people find the first day is the hardest, and you can step outside any time you need to.

What is the attendance policy?

  • If you arrive more than 20 minutes late or leave more than 20 minutes early, it counts as an absence.
  • TBLs, exams, lab quizzes and brain dumps happen in class and can't be made up, so missing one is a zero.
  • I treat every absence the same way. You never have to give me a reason or a doctor's note.
  • The doors lock when a lab practical starts, and anyone who is late can't come in.
  • Check Canvas at least three times a week, and turn on notifications for announcements.
  • Answer your team's messages within 24 hours.

Under college policy (Board Policy 5020), too many absences can get you dropped from the course.

Can I use AI?

You're welcome to use AI to study. The Teach It to an AI feature in Mastery OS is a good place to start.

For graded work, you can only use AI on assignments marked as AI assignments. You can't use it on exams or on any graded part of a TBL. If graded work is rated above 25% AI or plagiarism, it gets an automatic zero. If you're not sure whether a tool counts, ask me before you turn your work in.

The AI Honor Contract in Canvas has the full rules.

What counts as cheating?

Plagiarism means using someone else's work without giving them credit. That includes their words, ideas, images, diagrams and data, whether you meant to or not. On exams, cheating includes sharing information with another student, using materials that aren't allowed, and looking at someone else's exam.

Cheating earns at least a zero on that work. It can also mean an F in the course, and it's reported to the college. If you're not sure where the line is, ask me.

How much should I study?

15 to 17
hours a week outside class to pass with a C
25 to 26
hours a week outside class for most students who earn an A or B

These hours are on top of your time in class, which is about 8 hours a week.

Why so much? The usual guideline is about 2 hours of study for every hour of lecture. Lab usually doesn't add study time, because in most labs you do an experiment and you're done. Anatomy lab is different. You have to memorize what you see in lab for a timed practical, so both lecture and lab add study time.

You may need more if this is your first majors-level college science course or you haven't taken much science before. That's normal.

Make good use of your class time, especially in lab. The more you learn while you're in the room, the less you'll have to do at home, and the more of your study time can go to the more fun tools, like Loops, Kahoot and the games. Study a little every day instead of cramming, because anatomy builds on itself.
