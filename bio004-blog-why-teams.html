Teamwork

Why We Learn in Teams

Dr. Sharilyn Rennie · Aug 24, 2026 · 3 min read

Working in a team can be frustrating.

People have different schedules, priorities, personalities, and levels of preparation. Sometimes someone talks too much. Sometimes someone doesn't contribute enough. Sometimes you are depending on another person and they don't follow through.

I know that.

And that is actually part of the reason we work in teams.

If every team consisted of equally motivated people who communicated perfectly, divided the work fairly, always showed up prepared, and agreed with one another, there wouldn't be much to learn about teamwork.

The difficult parts are where some of the learning happens.

Think about the teams you have been part of in your life. Even the bad ones probably taught you something.

Maybe you learned what it feels like when someone doesn't follow through. Maybe you learned that one person can dominate a group even when that person isn't right. Maybe you learned that avoiding conflict doesn't solve conflict. Maybe you learned what kind of teammate you never want to be.

And sometimes you learn something about yourself. Every team experience gives you information about the kind of teammate you want to become and the kind of team you eventually want to help create.

Accountability feels different when other people are counting on you

When you work completely independently, you can procrastinate, show up unprepared, or give less than your best effort, and most of the consequences belong to you.

A team changes that. Now someone else is counting on you.

That can be uncomfortable. There is a certain freedom in knowing that if you choose not to prepare, you are the only person who pays for that decision. It feels very different when your choices affect four other people.

That is a lesson worth learning now.

In this class, being unprepared might mean your team struggles with a question. The consequences are relatively small.

In healthcare, they won't always be.

A nurse depends on another nurse for an accurate handoff. A physician depends on laboratory results. A respiratory therapist needs information from the rest of the care team. A surgeon depends on an entire operating room full of people doing their jobs well. The patient depends on all of them.

Healthcare is not individual work performed next to other people. It is interdependent work.

Your preparation affects someone else. Your communication affects someone else. Your willingness to speak up affects someone else. Your ability to listen when another person knows something you don't affects someone else.

That is why we start practicing now.

I don't expect you to love every team you are on. I don't expect every team to function perfectly. I do expect you to learn how to function within one.

Learn how to contribute. Learn how to listen. Learn how to disagree without making someone feel small. Learn how to handle frustration. Learn how to speak up when something isn't working. Learn how to recognize when you might be the person who needs to change something.

You are here to learn anatomy. Absolutely. But many of you are also preparing for professions where other people will depend on you. One day, someone may be counting on you for much more than a team score.

Learning how to be the person they can count on starts now.
